# Abstract

Inferring the directionality of gene flow from genetic data is a long-standing goal in evolutionary and landscape genetics, yet the measures most commonly employed—${F}_{ST}$ pairwise genetic distances and model-based migration rates—are either explicitly symmetric or depend on equilibrium assumptions routinely violated in the colonizing, fragmenting, and environmentally filtered systems where directional flow matters most. Population Graphs (Dyer & Nason, 2004\) recover the multivariate, conditional structure of genetic covariance as an undirected topology but do not, in canonical form, direct their edges. Here I introduce *genetic gravity*, a node-centric framework that converts a Population Graph’s undirected skeleton into a signed field of directional influence: for every retained edge, a pair of asymmetric weights ${w}_{i\rightarrow j}$ and ${w}_{j\rightarrow i}$ is derived from a Gaussian kernel over conditional genetic distances with a node-specific local bandwidth ${b}_{i}$, and their difference ${\Delta }_{ij}$ partitions the edge into directed components with no new connectivity and no free tuning parameter. Using stochastic individual-based simulations under isotropic and anisotropic migration, I characterize the index from single population-genetic snapshots: a hierarchy of significance tests matched to the inferential target; its specificity (it remains near zero under symmetric migration however much neutral differentiation accumulates); the sensitivity of its sign and rank to the bandwidth choice; its single-snapshot detection limits as functions of migration ratio and effective size; and its behaviour relative to the differentiation-based estimator divMigrate, which it complements while avoiding the latter’s drift-driven false positives and fixation artifacts. Genetic gravity offers a topology-derived, equilibrium-free measure of directional connectivity; its longitudinal dynamics are treated in a companion paper. All analyses are implemented in the gstudio R package.

# Introduction

Inferring the pattern and direction of genetic connectivity from molecular data is a central problem in evolutionary, population, and landscape genetics (Slatkin, 1985; Dyer, 2015).  The distribution of population genetic covariance we see today is the result of overlapping evolutionary processes—stochastic, neutral, and adaptive.  A common approach to elucidating the relative importance of these processes is to partition genetic variance into within- and among-stratum components using Wright’s *FST* (or one of its many derivatives).  Applying an underlying evolutionary model then allows us to compute the expected values of evolutionarily relevant parameters, such as migration rate (*m*; Slatkin 1981, 1993), genetic effective population size (*Ne*; XXX), inbreeding (*FIS*; XXXX), and others.  This process raises several specific challenges to using the current distribution of genetic variation to infer historical processes.  First, the relationship between a standardized parameter estimated and the stratigraphy of evolutionary and demographic histories that produced the set of sampled alleles and genotypes is non-reversible—the same ratio of within- and among-stratum variances can arise from completely non-overlapping demographic and evolutionary histories.   Second, treating connectivity as a scalar, undirected quantity is analytically convenient, but it homogenizes the varied histories of interactions that created these systems by taking an ‘average across generations under equilibrium conditions’ rather than directly embracing the potential for spatial and temporal heterogeneity within populations.  Estimating the effective number of migrants per generation, *Nem*, is critical for comparative inferences, but may be too coarse when the analytical questions focus on more intricate demographic processes or when attempting to include more granular landscape, demographic, or ecological features that may affect standing genetic structure.  Questions focusing on understanding how genetic structure is affected by colonization, range expansion, source–sink dynamics, wind- or water-mediated dispersal, and environmentally filtered gene flow benefit from approaches that are more fine-grained than a single summarizing parameter, as these inter-strata interactions are heterogeneous and fundamentally *directional* in nature (Baker & Dyer, 2011; Wogan et al., 2024).

The field is not, of course, without tools for addressing questions such as these, and an increasingly sophisticated set of approaches is available for estimating heterogeneity in connectivity and asymmetric gene flow from genetic data. Assignment-based tools such as BayesAss infer recent, directional migration rates from multilocus genotypes under linkage and Hardy–Weinberg assumptions (Wilson & Rannala, 2003), while coalescent and isolation-with-migration frameworks recover asymmetric long-term migration matrices, either by direct likelihood over genealogies (Beerli & Felsenstein, 2001\) or by fitting the joint site-frequency spectrum of a few populations (e.g., Gutenkunst et al., 2009). Where the question is specifically the direction of a range expansion, the directionality index $\psi$ reads the origin and axis of spread from pairwise allele-frequency asymmetries (Peter & Slatkin, 2013). At the landscape scale, effective-migration-surface methods map spatial heterogeneity in gene flow (Marcus et al., 2021; Petkova et al., 2016), and recent extensions of that family relax their symmetry assumption to render the surface directional. Each of these is powerful within its domain, but each also exacts a characteristic cost: a prespecified demographic or spatial model, an assumption of migration–drift equilibrium, genome-scale marker panels, a small number of demes, explicit spatial coordinates, or substantial computational resources. This manuscript provides a lightweight, assumption-free summary of *directional structure* that can be read directly from the multivariate covariance dependency topology and scales to many populations from a single snapshot. The framework extended herein is intended to fill this niche by using conditional genetic covariance structures, as represented within Population Graphs (Dyer & Nason, 2004; Dyer, 2007, 2015), to identify and quantify asymmetry in population migration.

Population Graphs offer a partial escape from the symmetry assumption using multilocus conditional genetic covariance within a graph-theoretic model. Rather than estimating a single pairwise statistic that describes the average among-stratum variance structure, the Population Graph framework translates the among-stratum variance into a weighted network of connectivity using conditional genetic covariance.  Population Graphs are distinct from pairwise structure or genetic distance analyses in one important way.  Pairwise approaches focus on estimates from each combinatorial pair of strata and, for example, reconstruct a relationship with pairwise distance (such as Slatkin’s $\hat{M}$ 19XX) to estimate isolation.  Conversely, estimates of stratum-to-stratum covariance in a Population Graph are based on the multilocus covariance between the two populations in question, statistically conditional on their covariance structures with the remaining populations in the analysis.  This resulting covariance topology captures dependencies that pairwise measures obscure. Moreover, the networks shape is diagnostic of the evolutionary processes that produced it—range expansions, vicariance, and equilibrium isolation-by-distance each leave characteristic signatures in node degree, edge length, and network diameter (Dyer, 2007, 2015). Population Graphs have been used to address questions of connectivity and structure in a broad range of scenarios including landscape genetics (Daniel et al., 2024; Dyer, 2015), conservation planning (Vilaça et al., 2024), biogeography (Wogan et al., 2024), life-history effects (Reed et al., 2023), invasive species management (Baker & Dyer, 2011), and pollen-mediated gene flow (Dileo et al., 2014; Dyer et al., 2004; Smouse et al., 2001).

The edges in a Population Graph are weighted estimates of conditional inter-population covariance: the conditional covariance ${e}_{ij}$ is a scalar, and the overall genetic covariance topology produced by the approach is, by definition, undirected.  Both source and sink populations may share a strong conditional dependency, but the *direction* of that dependency—who is overwriting whose allele frequencies—is a biologically distinct question that cannot be read directly from the undirected edge any more easily than it can be addressed with pairwise structure statistics. The same edge weight can be produced by a stable, symmetric exchange and by a unidirectional colonization pulse, yet the evolutionary implications are opposite, and in most cases, the actual inference of highest practical interest to the researcher.

Here, I introduce the concept of *genetic gravity*, an extension of the topology of the classic Population Graph that creates a node-centric framework for extracting directional information from an otherwise undirected Population Graph.  The goal here is to determine whether the conditional genetic structure encoded in the Population Graph topology itself can be used to partition symmetric covariance into asymmetric components, without invoking any new population-genetic model or incorporating additional outside information.  To this end,I begin by formalizing the notion of genetic-gravity deriving a pair of directional weights (${w}_{i\rightarrow j}$ and  ${w}_{j\rightarrow i}$) for each edge (${e}_{ij}$) to define an asymmetry index, ${\Delta }_{ij}$, using a node-local Gaussian kernel whose bandwidth is read directly from the Population Graph.  Second, I characterize the parameters' behavior—highlighting boundary effects that arise at low-degree nodes—under both stochastic models of symmetric and asymmetric connectivity. I validate the framework with stochastic individual-based simulations under three migration regimes—isotropic, flux-conserved anisotropic, and rate-conserved anisotropic—tracking both the canonical undirected Population Graph metrics and their directed counterparts derived from genetic gravity, as well as a mean asymmetry index $\Delta^{-}$. Next, I characterize the temporal dynamics and detectability of the directional signal against a within-replicate empirical null, using a standardized mixed-effects framework that explicitly models the strong autocorrelation of densely sampled graph trajectories.  Finally, I benchmark the asymmetry index against an existing approach suitable for the kinds of data found in the simulations using divMigrate, a widely used differentiation-based estimator of directional relative migration (Sundqvist et al., 2016), to show where the two agree and where the topology-derived index may be more specific in its inferences. All simulation routines and analysis functions are implemented in the gstudio R package maintained by the author and are freely available. Demonstration of the framework on empirical data and a bandwidth-sensitivity study are reserved for companion work.

# Methods

The directional framework proposed here assumes that a Population Graph has already been constructed from multilocus genetic data; the relevant inputs are the symmetric edge weights ${e}_{ij}$ and the adjacency structure of the graph $G=\{V,E\}$ retained by the construction of this Markov independence graph. Notation follows the *Q*\-mode formulation of Dyer et al. (2004) and the original Population Graph derivation in Dyer & Nason (2004); a compact summary sufficient for the derivation below is collected in the Appendix ([Table ](#bookmark=id.xx97r0kiirsn)A-1).

## **The Model of Genetic Gravity**

A Population Graph is a Markov independence graph and possesses several statistical features, two of which are relevant to the development of asymmetry models. First, a core property of all independence graphs concerns *graph separation*. Any pair of populations *directly* connected in a graph topology (e.g., $A\leftrightarrow B$ denoted by the edge ${e}_{AB}$) are, by definition, not genetically independent. Evolutionary processes that lead to conditional dependence include ongoing genetic connectivity or recent divergence from a common ancestral population.  Cessation of connectivity through time allows genetic drift and other processes to lead populations towards genetic independence. If populations are connected only *through* one or more other populations, in a stepwise fashion (e.g., $A\leftrightarrow B\leftrightarrow C$), populations not directly connected (such as $A$ and $C$) are, in population graphs, conditionally independent such that $P(A,C∣B)=P(A∣B)P(C∣B)$.

Second, nodes in a Population Graph have pairwise *Markov independence*, meaning that any two non-adjacent populations are conditionally independent given all other populations in the topology. If populations are added to or removed from the topology, the entire topology must be re-evaluated because the presence (and absence) of each edge depends on *all the data simultaneously*, not just the populations attached to that edge. This is one feature of Population Graphs that distinguishes them from more traditional pairwise structure estimators commonly used in evolutionary, landscape, and population genetic analyses. Markov independence is also reflexive: each target population is evolutionarily independent of all other populations in the topology, *except for* its direct neighbors. With these two properties, the estimators for asymmetry follow naturally.

## **Directional weights**

Each symmetric edge in a Population Graph is the pooled product of two conditional relationships: the contribution of population $j$ to the genetic structure of $i$, and the symmetric and opposite contribution of $i$ to $j$.  The edge weight, ${e}_{ij}$,  has been used as a measure of conditional genetic distance (cGD) to infer isolation by graph distance (Dyer 2015; Miles et al. 2018).

Focusing on each target node and its collection of neighboring edges ($N(i)$), I define a *directional weight* from node $i$ to node $j$ as the conditional probability that $j$ is the most influential neighbor of $i$ given the other nodes in $N(i)$ using a t-distribution Stochastic Neighborhood Embedding (tSNE; van der Maaten & Hinton 2008\) approach where:

$$ 
{w}_{i\rightarrow j}= \frac{exp​\left({- {e}_{ij}^{2}/2{b}_{i}^{2}}\right)}{\sum\limits_{k\in N(i)}^{}exp​\left({- {e}_{ik}^{2}/2{b}_{i}^{2}}\right)} 
$$ 

where ${e}_{ij}$ is the symmetric conditional-covariance edge weight from the Population Graph and ${b}_{i}$ is a node-specific *local bandwidth* parameter. The kernel is strictly local: only retained neighboring nodes, $j\in N(i)$ contribute to the denominator, so nodes that are conditionally independent of $i$ in $G$ receive zero weight by construction, preserving the Markov independence structure.

The bandwidth ${b}_{i}$ controls the rate at which the Gaussian kernel in [Equation 1](#bookmark=id.u6y1gk6p62kv) decays with distance: a large ${b}_{i}$ spreads influence broadly across the neighborhood, while a small ${b}_{i}$ concentrates it on the nearest neighbor. The choice of ${b}_{i}$ must be consistent with the Markov structure of the Population Graph, so the neighborhood edge weights $\{{e}_{ij}:j\in N(i)\}$ are the sufficient statistics for the scale of genetic signal decay at node $i$.  

I set the local bandwidth to the mean edge weight over node $i$’s retained neighborhood:

$${b}_{i} = \frac{{s}_{i}}{{k}_{i}} = \frac{1}{{k}_{i}}\sum\limits_{j\in N(i)}^{}{e}_{ij}$$					\[2\]

where ${s}_{i}=\sum\limits_{j\in N(i)}^{}{e}_{ij}$ is the *strength* (weighted degree) of node $i$. Intuitively, a node whose retained neighbors are on average genetically distant—whether because it occupies a bridging position between differentiated clusters or because it lies at the edge of a stepping-stone chain—receives a broad kernel that distributes its directional influence proportionally over that extended neighborhood, whereas a node embedded in a tight cluster of genetically similar populations receives a narrow kernel that concentrates its signal on its closest retained neighbors.  Moreover, this parameter is scale-equivariant: for any constant $c>0$, rescaling every conditional distance ${e}_{ij}↦c {e}_{ij}$ sends ${b}_{i}↦c {b}_{i}$, leaves each exponent ${e}_{ij}^{2}/2{b}_{i}^{2}$ fixed, and hence

$${w}_{i\rightarrow j}(c e)={w}_{i\rightarrow j}(e),  {\Delta }_{ij}(c e)={\Delta }_{ij}(e)  (c>0).$$			\[3\]

## **Asymmetric edge weights and the asymmetry index**

For every edge ${e}_{ij}\in E$, the directional weights ${w}_{i\rightarrow j}$ and ${w}_{j\rightarrow i}$ are computed independently from the two nodes’ local bandwidths:

$${w}_{i\rightarrow j}=\frac{exp(-{e}_{ij}^{2}/2{b}_{i}^{2})}{\sum\limits_{k\in N(i)}^{}exp(-{e}_{ik}^{2}/2{b}_{i}^{2})}  {w}_{j\rightarrow i}=\frac{exp(-{e}_{ij}^{2}/2{b}_{j}^{2})}{\sum\limits_{k\in N(j)}^{}exp(-{e}_{jk}^{2}/2{b}_{j}^{2})}$$

Because ${b}_{i}\ne {b}_{j}$ in general, the weights differ. The signed difference

$${\Delta }_{ij} = {w}_{i\rightarrow j}-{w}_{j\rightarrow i}$$						\[4\]

is the *asymmetry index*, interpretable as the net directional pressure exerted on edge $(i,j)$. Positive ${\Delta }_{ij}$ indicates that the edge contributes disproportionately more to the conditional structure of node $j$ than of node $i$—a signature consistent with $i$ acting as a source and $j$ as a sink. A value near zero indicates symmetric, balanced exchange; a large estimate of $|{\Delta }_{ij}|$ indicates a strongly directional edge.

It is worth being explicit about how a *directed* quantity can arise from an object that is symmetric by construction. The conditional-covariance structure underlying a Population Graph, and the edge weights ${e}_{ij}$ read from it, are symmetric (${e}_{ij}={e}_{ji}$); no manipulation of a single edge weight can yield a direction. The asymmetry in [Equation 4](#bookmark=id.aqg7easxaoi0) does not come from the edge—it comes from the two *neighborhoods the edge is compared against*. The local bandwidth ${b}_{i}$ is node $i$’s native genetic-distance scale, the mean conditional distance over its retained neighborhood ([Equation 2](#bookmark=id.m5kw6en0znro)), and the directional weight ${w}_{i\rightarrow j}$ measures how near edge $(i,j)$ is *relative to that scale*. The same edge weight ${e}_{ij}$ therefore reads as relatively near when $i$ sits in a diffuse, genetically distant neighborhood (large ${b}_{i}$) and relatively far when $j$ sits in a tight, genetically similar one (small ${b}_{j}$). The difference between these two relative readings, ${\Delta }_{ij}$, determines whether the edge is more typical of $i$’s local scale or of $j$’s. A symmetric edge embedded between two neighborhoods of *different scale* is genuinely asymmetric in this relational sense, and that asymmetry is information the symmetric matrix contains but that a pairwise reading of the edge in isolation discards. ## **Significance testing**

Assigning confidence to ${\Delta }_{ij}$ is complicated by the same topological dependence that gives the index its meaning. An edge is not an independent observation: it is constrained by its two endpoint nodes, which are in turn constrained by the entire graph. Worse yet, ${\Delta }_{ij}$ is built from the bandwidths ${b}_{i}$ and ${b}_{j}$, which are themselves functions of node degree and of the conditional-distance matrix $D$, so a parametric test would require the sampling distribution of a difference of normalized Gaussian kernels under conditional independence, which may be analytically intractable. Before choosing a procedure, however, one must be clear about *which* hypothesis is being tested, because “is there asymmetry?” is not a single question.

Here, I present a hierarchy of hypotheses, in decreasing order of statistical strength. (i) *Is there directional bias anywhere in the graph?*—a single omnibus contrast over the whole graph. (ii) *Is flow biased along a pre-specified axis, or within a pre-specified region?*—a design-driven, typically one-tailed test on an oriented (sub)graph, motivated by external knowledge such as downstream seed dispersal, prevailing wind, or an elevation gradient.  The design-driven middle level is often the most informative when sampling design is laid out to capture an expected directional axis and a single one-tailed contrast on that axis is both powerful and unambiguous.    
(iii) *Which individual edges or nodes are directional?*—an exploratory scan with one test per edge or node focusing on identifying heterogeneity in genetic connectivity across the topology. Depending on the spatial scale of sampling and the complexity of historical connectivity, there may be asymmetrical heterogeneity spread across the landscape.

The first two levels share one simple statistic. For each edge, the pairwise-normalized arc weight ${\tilde{w}}_{i\rightarrow j}={w}_{i\rightarrow j}/({w}_{i\rightarrow j}+{w}_{j\rightarrow i})$ is the share of the edge’s directional weight pointing in a coincident direction. It is scale-free—the conditional distance cancels—so it isolates direction from magnitude. The natural reference is symmetry, $\tilde{w}=\frac{1}{2}$, and the test is then a one-sample, one-tailed nonparametric test (sign or Wilcoxon signed-rank) of the signed departures $\tilde{w}-\frac{1}{2}$ over the edges, oriented along the a priori axis: the omnibus statistic over all edges, or the design-driven statistic over a chosen induced subgraph. It may be tempting to subtract this degree-expected center, but the operative null center is whatever a *symmetric* process yields, and directional gene flow itself reshapes node degrees along a specific axis, suggesting the retained edge set is not invariant under the treatment.  Centering on the observed graph’s degree therefore subtracts part of the signal; empirically it inverts the flux-conserved scenario’s sign, whereas the empirical isotropic center sits very close to one-half because the symmetric null has a balanced degree. For simplicity, I therefore center on one-half (equivalently, on the empirical symmetric-null center), not on the observed degree. Collapsing the graph to a single directional contrast removes the multiple-testing burden of a per-edge scan and maximizes power for a pre-registered hypothesis. It does *not*, however, escape edge dependence: the arc weights on a single graph share nodes, so a textbook signed-rank or sign-test variance estimate is understated, and the nominal *p*\-value is anti-conservative.  To correct for this, the significance must be referenced to a dependence-respecting null—the empirical isotropic null used in the simulation study, or a structure-preserving per-graph permutation (the bandwidth-alignment null below)—rather than to a nominal nonparametric variance.  I provide code to estimate the statistic and to create resampled null distributions based on your topology in the gstudio R package (see Software and data availability).

The exploratory level (iii) and any single observed graph for which one wants per-edge confidence calls for a resampling null, and the appropriate one depends on which nuisance is being controlled. I describe the procedure I recommend for edge-level inference, then note three further options and where each applies.

A natural first instinct—permuting individual labels across strata and simply re-estimating the whole Population Graph each time—turns out to be a poor test of the *magnitude* of ${\Delta }_{ij}$ on a particular edge, for two reasons:

1. The **denominator problem**: re-running graph construction allows the topology to change from permutation to permutation, so a given edge may be retained in only a fraction of the null graphs, leaving a small and unstable null sample for that edge.  

2. The **confounded significance**: a “significant” result then conflates two distinct things—whether the asymmetry is large, and whether the edge itself is rare under the null—so one cannot say which drove the outcome.

Testing ${\Delta }_{ij}$ on the edges one actually has requires holding the topology fixed and randomizing only the genetic “mass” that generates the asymmetry.

The **fixed-topology permutation test** does exactly this and is the principal resampling tool for edge-level significance. The adjacency of the observed Population Graph is held fixed; individual labels are permuted across strata to generate null centroid configurations and hence a null conditional-distance matrix; and ${b}_{i}$ and ${\Delta }_{ij}$ are recomputed *on the original edge set*. A two-tailed *p*\-value for each edge is the fraction of permutations yielding $|{\Delta }_{null}|\geq |{\Delta }_{obs}|$, with the conventional add-one correction so that no *p*\-value is exactly zero. Because every retained edge is present in every permutation, each edge receives a full, equally sized null distribution—the denominator problem disappears—and the test isolates the contribution of the populations’ genetic identities from that of their fixed positions in the graph. The defining feature is that the *adjacency* is never recomputed: the edge set $E$ is reused unchanged across permutations, and only the edge weights are regenerated from the permuted centroids. One limitation should be stated plainly: because individuals are permuted across *all* strata, the reference is the no-structure (panmixia) null—the same null that AMOVA and ${F}_{ST}$ use for ${\sigma }_{A}^{2}=0$—rather than a symmetric-*structure* null. It therefore reproduces the topological component of $\Delta$ only partially, leaving a mild, differentiation-independent anti-conservative offset ([Section 3.5](#bookmark=id.khqknek1q2u2)). The bandwidth-alignment null below, which holds the metric geometry fixed and permutes only the node bandwidths, conditions on that geometry and is the better-calibrated choice when this offset matters.

The **stability test** (jackknife or bootstrap) answers a different question: not whether an asymmetry exceeds the graph’s geometry, but whether it is robust to the particular individuals sampled. Individuals are resampled within each stratum, the Population Graph is re-estimated, and ${\Delta }_{ij}$ is recalculated; the spread across resamples gives a confidence interval for each edge, and a ${\Delta }_{ij}$ whose 95% interval excludes zero is directionally stable. This is the appropriate check when the concern is that a few outlier individuals—rather than a population-level process—are driving an apparent signal of expansion.

Two further nulls are worth noting but are narrower or broader in scope. Permuting the ${b}_{i}$ values across nodes while holding both $D$ and the adjacency fixed targets the *alignment* of bandwidths: it asks whether the specific pairing of source- and sink-like nodes across an edge is non-random, isolating the bandwidth assignment from distance and topology. At the opposite extreme, comparing against rewired graphs of the same size or degree distribution—a network null—tests whether the *whole configuration* of asymmetry is unusual; but for a specific edge, it is typically too conservative, because that edge vanishes in most rewirings and the test collapses into a composite of whether the edge exists *and* is asymmetric. For inference about the edges of a graph one has already accepted as significant, the fixed-topology test is preferable. These tests are intended for directional inference on a single empirical Population Graph (the software used to implement them is described in the Software and data availability section). The simulation study that follows takes a complementary approach: rather than testing one observed graph, it characterizes how the index behaves under known migration regimes across many replicate snapshots.

## **Individual-based simulations**

To evaluate the sensitivity and temporal behavior of the asymmetry index under known migration regimes, I implemented a two-phase stochastic, individual-based simulation following the approach of R. Dyer (2007). Each replicate tracks a metapopulation of $K=25$ discrete locales, each with $N=100$ diploid individuals genotyped at $L=20$ independently segregating codominant, biallelic loci. Initial allele frequencies were drawn independently for each locus from ${p}_{ℓ}\sim N(0.5, 0.1)$, clamped to the interval $[0.01,0.99]$, with ${q}_{ℓ}=1-{p}_{ℓ}$, and shared identically across all populations (common-ancestor start); allele frequencies were uncorrelated across loci. Random mating within populations produced non-overlapping generations; mutation was not included in this treatment, which isolates the drift–migration dynamics of interest but means that allelic diversity erodes slowly under drift over the simulated horizon—a constraint that bounds the interpretable window and is revisited in the Discussion. Fifty independent replicates were run, each yielding a distinct realization of all three treatment scenarios from a shared burn-in state. Simulations were implemented in R using the gstudio package (R. Dyer, 2013).

### **Phase 1—Isotropic burn-in**

Every replicate, regardless of treatment, begins with 2,000 generations of symmetric gene flow on a one-dimensional stepping-stone topology. Adjacent populations exchange migrants at the per-direction rate ${m}_{i\rightarrow i+1}={m}_{i+1\rightarrow i}=0.025$, giving a total per-population emigration rate of $m=0.05$ for all interior nodes. End populations exchange migrants only with their single neighbor at the same per-direction rate, so their total emigration rate is $0.025$.  
Separating the burn-in phase from the directional treatment allows the populations in each simulation to achieve a drift–migration equilibrium with respect to genetic covariance (see R. Dyer, 2007 for examples). Allowing populations to reach equilibrium before imposing asymmetric gene flow provides a clean $t=0$ reference point and supports the sensitivity analyses on the evolution of asymmetric structure described below. The 2,000-generation burn-in is well past the equilibrium plateau for all graph-theoretic metrics, which I verify empirically through the saturation analysis in the Results.

### **Phase 2—Treatment scenarios**

At the end of burn-in ($t=0$ at generation 2,000), the migration matrix is replaced by one of three scenario-specific matrices. The topology and population sizes are otherwise unchanged. In all three scenarios, “forward” denotes the direction of increasing population index ($i\rightarrow i+1$) and “reverse” denotes the opposite ($i+1\rightarrow i$), with boundary populations adjusted to a single neighbor.

1. **Isotropic continuation:** ${m}_{i\rightarrow i+1}={m}_{i+1\rightarrow i}=0.025$. Migration continues at the burn-in rate without any change in structure. This scenario yields the null distribution of ${\Delta }_{ij}$ at equilibrium: any nonzero asymmetry index values arise solely from finite-sample stochasticity and drift. It supplies the within-replicate empirical null and the reference variance against which the treatment effects in Scenarios 2 and 3 are measured, and—because it is a true symmetric-migration null—it is also the natural substrate for calibrating the false-positive rate of the fixed-topology permutation test (demonstrated across a differentiation gradient in [Section 3.5](#bookmark=id.khqknek1q2u2)).  

2. **Flux-conserved asymmetric:** ${m}_{i\rightarrow i+1}=0.040$, ${m}_{i+1\rightarrow i}=0.010$, so ${m}_{i\rightarrow i+1}+{m}_{i+1\rightarrow i}=0.050=m$. The total emigration rate from each interior population is held constant at the burn-in value: the same proportion of each population disperses each generation, but four times as many emigrants travel forward as travel in reverse. Constraining the total flux preserves the per-population drift dynamics—effective size, within-population heterozygosity, and average rate of allele-frequency change are all unchanged from the burn-in. Any systematic change in ${\Delta }_{ij}$ following $t=0$ is therefore attributable to the directional redistribution of flux alone, not to any change in the overall rate of genetic exchange. This is the most conservative of the three treatment scenarios.  

3. **Rate-conserved asymmetric:** ${m}_{i\rightarrow i+1}=0.025$, ${m}_{i+1\rightarrow i}=0.010$, so the forward rate is identical to the isotropic baseline while back-flow is suppressed by 60%. Total emigration from interior populations ($0.035$) is lower than during the burn-in, indicating that populations retain slightly more local ancestry each generation. This scenario models the ecologically common situation in which dispersal in one direction—downstream, downwind, or along a prevailing current—proceeds at the ambient rate while return dispersal is partially obstructed by a landscape feature or behavioral asymmetry. Because only the reverse rate differs from the null, the scenario isolates the contribution of back-flow suppression to *directional signal* independently of any enhancement of forward flux.

The three scenarios form a partial factorial over the two directional rates, which permits decomposition of the drivers of the asymmetry index ([Table 3](#bookmark=id.1naezah9bfi)). Scenarios 1 and 2 share the same total flux ($m=0.05$) but differ in directional balance (1:1 versus 4:1), so their contrast isolates the effect of flux redistribution while holding total emigration constant. Scenarios 1 and 3 share the same forward rate (${m}_{i\rightarrow i+1}=0.025$) but differ in reverse rate ($0.025$ versus $0.010$), so their contrast isolates the effect of back-flow suppression. Scenarios 2 and 3 share the same reverse rate (${m}_{i+1\rightarrow i}=0.010$) but differ in forward rate ($0.040$ versus $0.025$), so their contrast isolates the incremental effect of forward-rate elevation beyond the isotropic baseline. Together, the three scenarios answer three linked questions: (i) can ${\Delta }_{ij}$ detect directionality when the only change is the direction of flux, not its magnitude? (ii) Does back-flow suppression alone generate a detectable asymmetric signal? And (iii) does elevating the forward rate above the ambient level accelerate or amplify the signal relative to mere back-flow suppression?

## **Graph summaries**

Each forward snapshot is converted into a Population Graph, and for each retained edge, the directional weights and the asymmetry index in [Equation 4](#bookmark=id.aqg7easxaoi0) are computed. From each graph, I record the number of retained edges, the mean conditional genetic distance, and the mean asymmetry index $\Delta ‾$; these per-snapshot summaries are the inputs to the analyses below. The construction of *directed* graph-theoretic metrics from the same arc weights, along with their longitudinal behavior, is developed in the companion paper.

## **Comparison with a differentiation-based directional method**

To place the asymmetry index in the context of an existing tool that could be applied to data like these, I compared it directly with divMigrate (Sundqvist et al., 2016), which estimates directional relative migration between population pairs from genetic differentiation and is among the most widely used network-level descriptors of asymmetric gene flow. For the final-generation Population Graph of each replicate and scenario, I computed divMigrate’s directional relative migration ${m}_{i\rightarrow j}$ (using Jost’s $D$ as the underlying differentiation statistic) and formed the edge-level divMigrate asymmetry ${A}_{ij}={m}_{i\rightarrow j}-{m}_{j\rightarrow i}$ on the same retained, oriented edges as ${\Delta }_{ij}$, so the two indices are directly comparable. They are then contrasted in two ways: their per-edge *concordance* under the asymmetric scenarios (Spearman correlation, testing whether the two agree on the direction and rank of the imposed signal) and their *specificity* under the isotropic null (testing which index more cleanly returns to zero when migration is symmetric). Because divMigrate derives its directionality from pairwise differentiation rather than from the conditional graph topology, it is expected to be the more sensitive of the two to drift- and ${N}_{e}$\-driven differentiation that is not directional in origin. To compare the two indices through time rather than at the endpoint alone, I repeated the edge-level comparison at a grid of forward generations and used it to assess each index’s *single-snapshot sensitivity*—how reliably one replicate’s snapshot separates an asymmetric scenario from the symmetric null—analyzed in [Section 3.2](#bookmark=id.z8zzpuz0u5e3).

## **Comparison with divMigrate**

T[able 4](#bookmark=id.d0s1rdojvfcw) places the two indices side by side. Under both asymmetric scenarios, the per-edge concordance is positive (median Spearman $\rho$ \= \-0.054 flux-conserved, 0.036 rate-conserved): where the simulation imposes a genuine directional signal, divMigrate and the asymmetry index agree on which edges are directional and on their orientation. The two diverge under the isotropic null, and there the contrast is one of *specificity*. With migration perfectly symmetric, the asymmetry index sits at its empirical near-zero baseline (mean $|\Delta |$ \= 0.0329), whereas divMigrate reports a substantially larger residual directional signal (mean $|A|$ \= 0.053, roughly 1\.6$\times$ larger) that arises not from directional gene flow but from the drift- and ${N}_{e}$\-driven differences in differentiation that its pairwise construction cannot separate from direction (in the extreme, strong directional flow drives the allele fixation that destabilizes divMigrate’s differentiation normalization altogether—a failure mode induced by asymmetry itself, documented in [Section 3.2.1](#bookmark=id.h2b9jyq8vtp8)). The comparison therefore supports a complementary reading: the two methods recover the same directional structure when it is present, but the topology-derived index is less prone of the two to mistaking nondirectional differentiation for directional flow—the property that matters most when the analyst does not already know whether asymmetry is present. The same contrast cast as a single-snapshot *sensitivity* question—how reliably one replicate’s snapshot separates an asymmetric scenario from the symmetric null, and how that sensitivity builds over the generations following onset—is reported in [Section 3.2](#bookmark=id.z8zzpuz0u5e3).

## **Sensitivity to asymmetry: genetic gravity versus divMigrate**

The single-generation comparison in [Section 3.1](#bookmark=id.ebqbpzur3vdc) shows that, at the end of the forward phase, genetic gravity and divMigrate agree on the edges where directional flow is genuinely present. The more practical question, however, is one of *sensitivity*: in the real world, an analyst has a single snapshot, not fifty, and must decide from that one dataset whether asymmetry is present. I therefore treat each replicate’s snapshot as an independent trial—exactly the situation a single study occupies—and ask how reliably each index distinguishes an asymmetric scenario from the symmetric null. Because the simulation contains the symmetric null directly (the isotropic arm), no internal permutation is required: at each forward generation, the isotropic replicates supply the empirical distribution of each index’s single-snapshot magnitude statistic (mean $|\Delta |$ for gravity, mean $|A|$ for divMigrate), against which the asymmetric replicates are scored. I summarize the result in two ways: the rank-based area under the ROC curve (AUC), a threshold-free measure of separation that—being a function only of ordering—is immune to the divMigrate ${D}_{max}$ blow-up; and the power at a fixed 5% false-positive rate, taking the isotropic 95th percentile as the decision threshold so that specificity is pinned identically for both methods. The focus is the first few hundred generations after onset, where the gravity curves rise toward their plateau and where divMigrate’s late-generation fixation artifact has not yet set in.

At the stabilized window—about 200 generations after onset—the two indices behave very differently ([Figure 1](#bookmark=id.wo7yc5p4zxiq), [Table 5](#bookmark=id.be77jb5uuotw)). Genetic gravity separates the asymmetric scenarios from the symmetric null, strongly under flux-conserved connectivity (AUC 0.664, power 0.18 at a 5% false-positive rate, with the AUC climbing past 0.8 over the following generations) and weakly under the slower rate-conserved regime (AUC 0.502, near chance at this point but rising thereafter; power 0.16). The gravity curves are also non-monotonic in the first hundred-odd generations, dipping toward or below the 0.5 no-discrimination line before climbing ([Table 5](#bookmark=id.be77jb5uuotw), [Figure 1](#bookmark=id.wo7yc5p4zxiq))—an inheritance of the transient, reversing responses of the underlying graph metrics themselves, so a single very early snapshot can momentarily sit at chance even though the directional signal strengthens with time. The multiple checkpoints in [Table 5](#bookmark=id.be77jb5uuotw) make that timing explicit, spanning the early reversal as well as the later plateau. divMigrate’s magnitude statistic, by contrast, does not discriminate at all: its AUC sits at or below the 0.5 no-information line in both scenarios (0.198 flux-conserved, 0.34 rate-conserved) and its power stays at the 5% chance rate (0 and 0.04). The sub-0.5 AUC is itself diagnostic. It means divMigrate’s edge-asymmetry magnitude tends to be *larger* under symmetric migration than under the asymmetric scenarios, because $|A|$ tracks the overall amount of pairwise differentiation—which the higher-connectivity flux-conserved regime actually lowers—rather than its directionality. This is the single-snapshot face of the specificity problem seen at the endpoint ([Section 3.1](#bookmark=id.ebqbpzur3vdc)): the size of divMigrate’s directional signal is governed by how much differentiation drift has produced, not by whether that differentiation is directional. (The point concerns using the *magnitude* of $|A|$ as evidence that asymmetry is present; where the directional signal is strong, divMigrate can still rank edge orientations correctly, as the positive endpoint concordance showed.) Because the AUC is rank-based and the power threshold is pinned at the isotropic 95th percentile, neither readout is driven by the late-generation normalization blow-up ([Section 3.2.1](#bookmark=id.h2b9jyq8vtp8)); the contrast is a genuine difference in how cleanly each statistic isolates directional signal from the drift- and ${N}_{e}$\-driven scatter that a symmetric metapopulation also produces.

F[igure 2](#bookmark=id.vsx2vmdxmttx) shows the raw signal that the sensitivity analysis scores: the across-replicate mean of each index, expressed relative to its paired isotropic value, grows away from the symmetric baseline under both scenarios, confirming that the directional signal is present in each. The sensitivity curves above quantify how reliably that growing signal can be recovered from a *single* replicate, which is the inference an analyst actually faces.

### **A failure mode induced by asymmetry itself**

A further pattern in [Figure 2](#bookmark=id.vsx2vmdxmttx) is worth recording in its own right, because it is a downstream *consequence* of asymmetric gene flow rather than incidental noise: at late generations 38% of divMigrate’s aggregated time-points fall outside the gravity-index display range by many orders of magnitude (up to $\sim {10}^{13}$). The divergence itself is numerical—it originates in divMigrate’s normalization—but it is *triggered by a genuine biological process set in motion by the asymmetry being studied*. The method normalizes pairwise directional relative migration as ${m}_{i\rightarrow j}=1-{D}_{ij}/{D}_{max}$, where ${D}_{max}$ is the largest Jost’s $D$ observed across all population pairs. Under the rate-conserved scenario, the sustained directional drift drives near-complete allele fixation in a subset of replicates at late forward generations (approximately 75% of loci per population fully fixed in affected replicates vs 63% in unaffected replicates at the same generation), collapsing differentiation across most pairs toward zero and pulling ${D}_{max}$ toward zero. When the normalization denominator is near zero, any residual differentiation on a small number of edges produces ${m}_{i\rightarrow j}\gg 1$, and the resulting edge asymmetry ${A}_{ij}={m}_{i\rightarrow j}-{m}_{j\rightarrow i}$ diverges to extreme values. The broader lesson is that strong directional gene flow is exactly the regime in which a differentiation-normalized directional estimator becomes least stable—the asymmetry the analyst is trying to measure is also what erodes the differentiation signal the estimator depends on—whereas a topology-derived index is structurally insulated from this feedback. This artifact does not contaminate the sensitivity comparison: the AUC is a function only of rank order, so a blown-up value counts simply as “larger than the isotropic replicates” and contributes no more than any other ordering; and the power comparison fixes the decision threshold at the isotropic 95th percentile, so the realized false-positive rate is held at 5% regardless of the divMigrate scale. Restricting the sensitivity window to the first few hundred generations further sidesteps the issue, since the fixation that drives the blow-up is a late-generation phenomenon. The gravity index is entirely unaffected in any case, because it derives from the conditional independence structure of the Population Graph rather than from any pairwise differentiation ratio.

## **Bandwidth sensitivity**

The bandwidth estimator ${b}_{i}={s}_{i}/{k}_{i}$ is a modeling choice; the load-bearing question is not its absolute scale but whether the *sign* and *rank order* of ${\Delta }_{ij}$—which edge flows which way, and which edges carry the strongest signal—survive a deliberately non-local choice. I recompute $\Delta$ on the base-study forward snapshots under three departures from the canonical local bandwidth and compare each, edge by edge, to it: a fixed *global* bandwidth (a single scale, the mean retained edge weight, for every node); the local bandwidth *scaled* by $\frac{1}{2}$ and by $2$ (a sharper and a flatter kernel); and a *perplexity*\-based per-node bandwidth (a tSNE-style $\sigma$ search). All are evaluated on the comparable ${w}_{i\rightarrow j}-{w}_{j\rightarrow i}$ scale. Against the canonical index I record, over the directional scenarios, the fraction of edges with the same sign, the Spearman correlation of the signed $\Delta$ and of $|\Delta |$, and whether the graph-mean $\Delta ‾$ keeps its sign.

The result is more nuanced than a clean robustness verdict ([Table 6](#bookmark=id.7qvydg2a0xw4)), and it separates the aggregate from the edge level. Edge-level direction is only *moderately* preserved: across the four non-local alternatives, the sign of ${\Delta }_{ij}$ agrees with the canonical local estimator on 75–84% of edges—so roughly one edge in five changes orientation—and the signed rank order correlates at Spearman $\rho =0.38$–0.78, weakest for the perplexity kernel (a genuinely different construction rather than a rescaling) and imperfect even for the mild $\times \frac{1}{2}$ and $\times 2$ rescalings. The graph-mean $\Delta ‾$ is steadier than the edge level: its sign is preserved across the alternatives in 87% of flux-conserved and 84% of rate-conserved snapshots, the aggregate orientation surviving bandwidth changes that reshuffle individual edges. The reading is therefore that the bandwidth is a genuine modeling choice at the edge level, not an immaterial convenience: changing it both rescales magnitudes and reorders and reorients a meaningful minority of edges. What is robust is the *aggregate* directional signal—$\Delta ‾$ and the longitudinal graph-metric trajectories of the main text, on which the conclusions rest—while individual-edge orientations are bandwidth-sensitive and should be read as exploratory, consistent with the edge non-independence and degree dependence documented in [Section 3.6](#bookmark=id.lnhlrjkgu1n) and [Section 4.4](#bookmark=id.uxcs9kkexnw7). The local estimator remains the principled default (scale-equivariant and parameter-free), but it earns that status by argument rather than by the edge-level invariance this check was expected to show.

## **Boundary effects and the drift–migration balance at chain termini**

The asymmetry index carries a topological component at low-degree nodes (the leaf-node effect of [Section 4.4](#bookmark=id.uxcs9kkexnw7)). Because the simulated stepping stone has two degree-limited termini, two questions follow: whether that component biases the directional signal, and—since the same termini are the most drift-exposed demes in the system—whether the boundary is better read as a genuine evolutionary regime than as a numerical edge case. I address both by decomposing the forward-phase snapshots by chain position, separating edges that touch a degree-one terminus from interior edges, and tracking expected heterozygosity at the ends versus the interior.

Decomposing the index by edge class separates the topological boundary effect from the directional signal ([Table 7](#bookmark=id.24c8dlkr5tn1)). Leaf-incident edges—those touching a degree-one terminus, where one directional weight is forced to $1$—carry a large mean $|\Delta |$ (about NaN) that is essentially the same across all three scenarios, the isotropic null included: this is the geometry talking, not gene flow, and it is exactly the component the fixed-topology permutation test reproduces in its own null and therefore calibrates away (Methods). The interior edges tell the opposite story. With the leaf edges removed, the mean asymmetry still separates the scenarios at roughly 304 generations after onset (interior $\Delta ‾$ \= \-0.018 flux-conserved, \-0.007 rate-conserved, 0.001 isotropic), at a smaller typical magnitude (about 0.038) than the leaf edges. The directional signal is therefore an interior, distributed property of the graph rather than an inflation manufactured at the boundary: the periphery contributes magnitude but no scenario discrimination.

That the boundary contributes magnitude is itself biologically meaningful, not merely a nuisance. A terminus deme exchanges with a single neighbor and so receives roughly half the immigrant input of an interior deme; its drift–migration balance is tipped toward drift, and it loses variation faster. The simulations show exactly this ([Figure 3](#bookmark=id.6hmzv24iwhhu)): expected heterozygosity at the chain ends sits below the interior in every scenario and falls fastest under rate-conserved connectivity, where the lower total migration (0.035 versus 0.050) compounds the peripheral isolation—directional flow plus reduced total exchange let drift dominate and burn down the variation the graph is built from (terminus ${H}_{e}$ ≈ 0.089 versus interior 0.094 by the end of the window). This is the same process, seen at the periphery, that collapses divMigrate’s ${D}_{max}$ at late generations ([Section 3.2.1](#bookmark=id.h2b9jyq8vtp8)): a differentiation-based estimator runs out of signal precisely where drift has run polymorphism down, whereas the topology-derived index keeps reading the conditional structure that remains. The leaf-node behavior of ${\Delta }_{ij}$ is thus not only a geometric artifact to be calibrated away but a marker of the genuine evolutionary regime at a stepping-stone terminus: weak immigration, strong drift, and an unambiguous source- or sink-terminus polarity on the gradient.

### **Specificity: differentiation without direction**

The framework also claims *specificity*: the asymmetry index should respond to the *direction* of gene flow, not to the *amount* of genetic structure. Stream C, Arm A tests this with a gradient of symmetric (1:1) scenarios at progressively lower migration—conditions that tip the drift–migration balance toward drift and raise neutral differentiation without imposing any direction. I test specificity at two levels, beginning with the rigorous one. The self-contained statistical test asks whether the per-edge significance procedure is *calibrated*: under symmetric migration, an edge flagged as directional is a false positive, and a well-behaved test should flag such edges at the nominal rate, regardless of how much neutral differentiation has accumulated. I run the fixed-topology permutation test (Methods) on a sample of symmetric-scenario snapshots spanning the gradient and record, per snapshot, the fraction of edges with $p<\alpha$; because the adjacency is held fixed and only the centroids are permuted, the geometric component of ${\Delta }_{ij}$ is reproduced in each edge’s null, so under symmetry the empirical false-positive rate should not rise with differentiation (and, where that component is fully absorbed, should sit near $\alpha$).

Over 499 permutations per snapshot (20 snapshots per scenario), the empirical edge-level false-positive rate is essentially flat across the differentiation gradient ([Table 8](#bookmark=id.pww9fwqotcjy), [Figure 4](#bookmark=id.y9nq0j7n12cg)): as mean differentiation rises about 2.1-fold from the isotropic baseline to the lowest-migration arm, the FPR at $\alpha =0.05$ stays within 0.061–0.089, and is in fact slightly lower at the most-differentiated arm. The rate does sit modestly above nominal—roughly 1.5× at $\alpha =0.05$ and somewhat more at the stringent $\alpha =0.01$ (about 2.8×)—but that offset is differentiation-independent (its source examined below), not neutral structure being read as direction. The decisive property is the invariance: holding the adjacency fixed and permuting only the centroid identities, differentiation alone does not inflate the false-positive rate, so neutral structure—however extreme—is not manufactured into significant directional edges. This substantiates the Scenario-1 false-positive claim in the Methods as a demonstration of differentiation robustness. The offset is not a pendant-edge artifact: restricting to interior edges (dropping those incident to a degree-one node) leaves the $\alpha =0.05$ rate essentially unchanged at 0.061–0.089 (about 1\.5$\times$ nominal), so the mild anti-conservatism is distributed across the graph rather than localized to the topologically forced leaf edges. (gstudio exposes a pendant argument to flag or drop leaf-incident edges, whose direction is forced by topology and is best interpreted with care.) The mean and median conditional distances are close (mean/median $\approx$ 1.08, [Table 8](#bookmark=id.pww9fwqotcjy)), so the per-edge weight distribution is roughly symmetric and edge-weight outliers are not the driver. A second null isolates the cause. The bandwidth-alignment permutation—which holds the metric geometry (distances and adjacency) fixed and permutes only the node bandwidths, testing directional *alignment* rather than the presence of any structure—yields a $\alpha =0.05$ false-positive rate of 0.013–0.021 (about 0.3 $\times$ nominal). It is therefore *conservative* where the label permutation is anti-conservative: by conditioning on the geometry that label permutation destroys, it errs toward non-detection rather than toward false significance—the safer direction for a screening test. Neither null is exactly calibrated (the precisely calibrated reference is the empirical isotropic null used in the simulation study), but the contrast localizes the label permutation’s offset to its panmixia reference: removing it overshoots into conservatism rather than landing on nominal.

The same specificity is evident in the graph-mean index $\Delta ‾$, a descriptive complement to the calibrated per-edge test above. If the index were merely a differentiation detector, $\Delta ‾$ it would climb with the gradient; if it is direction-specific, it should stay at the isotropic null, however high differentiation rises—the “scissors” of [Figure 5](#bookmark=id.1ed05cgbjyoj).

Across the symmetric gradient, the drift–migration balance tips steadily toward drift: as the per-direction rate falls, neutral differentiation climbs sharply—mean cGD at sym-verylow reaches roughly 2.6× its isotropic value—yet the *signed* mean asymmetry index never leaves the isotropic null, staying below 9e-04 across the entire gradient with no trend in sign ([Figure 5](#bookmark=id.1ed05cgbjyoj), [Table 9](#bookmark=id.b5j4758edgjf)). That is the scissors the metric is designed to show: the *amount* of structure rises sharply while its *direction* does not, because symmetric migration produces no systematic neighborhood-scale imbalance for the kernel to read. The absolute index |$\Delta ‾$| does creep up modestly (from 0.0047 under isotropy to about 0.0058 at sym-verylow) as differentiation rises—an expected variance effect, since noisier per-edge weights inflate the magnitude of a mean that remains centered on zero—but it stays well below the levels the asymmetric scenarios reach. Those directional scenarios depart from the null at differentiation far below what this gradient reaches, so the index responds to directionality, not to differentiation per se—the within-method counterpart of the endpoint contrast with divMigrate ([Section 3.1](#bookmark=id.ebqbpzur3vdc)).

## **The design-driven directional test in practice**

The calibration above concerns the per-edge permutation null; here, I ask whether the design-driven contrast in Table 1 can be read off a *single* graph using a nonparametric test—the natural thing a practitioner on a 1-D system might reach for. I apply the arc-weight directional test (one-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank, centered on symmetry, edges oriented by ascending stepping-stone position) to each replicate’s final-generation graph ($N=100$, generation 2999\) under the three base-study scenarios, treating its built-in *p*\-value at face value. The isotropic scenario gauges the false-positive rate of that single-snapshot test; the two asymmetric scenarios gauge its power.

Centered on symmetry, the location bias is gone—the isotropic median forward bias is \+0.004—but the single-snapshot test still over-rejects, flagging 18% of isotropic replicates against a nominal 5% ([Table 10](#bookmark=id.ar339dsfruom)). The residual is a variance failure attributed below to edge dependence. The two asymmetric scenarios push direction oppositely along the axis, as in the main text: flux-conserved shifts the arc weights downstream (median forward bias \+0.067) and is detected by the forward one-tailed test in 52% of single snapshots, while rate-conserved shifts them the other way (median \-0.037) and is therefore not flagged by a forward-only test (0%). The one-tailed alternative must match the hypothesized direction; a two-sided test, or the correct sign, recovers the second scenario. One caution holds regardless of centering: the roughly 87 edges of a single graph share nodes—each $\tilde{w}$ is built from its endpoints’ whole neighborhoods—so a textbook signed-rank variance treats non-independent observations as independent and the nominal *p*\-value is anti-conservative. The statistic is sound as a directional summary, but rigorous single-graph inference should reference it to the empirical isotropic null used throughout this study, or to a structure-preserving permutation (the bandwidth-alignment null of Table 2), rather than to the nominal nonparametric variance.

## **Detection limits: The operating envelope**

Stream C, Arm B asks the complementary question—not whether a false signal appears under symmetry, but how strong a *true* directional signal must be, relative to drift, before the method can detect it from a single snapshot. It sweeps the asymmetry ratio (down to 1.5:1) against effective size ${N}_{e}$ (via $N\in \{50,100,200,400\}$) under the flux-conserved convention, and scores single-snapshot power against the matched 1:1 null at each ${N}_{e}$. A natural population-genetic hypothesis is that detectability is governed by the composite of signal and drift ($\sim 4{N}_{e} \Delta m$) rather than by the ratio or ${N}_{e}$ alone; the grid tests whether the operating envelope collapses onto that composite axis or instead tracks one factor more directly.At $N=50$ the finite-sample drift–migration dynamics expose a limit specific to Population Graph construction. A Population Graph is estimated from the conditional genetic covariance among populations; as drift drives alleles toward fixation, that covariance degenerates, retained edges drop away, and past some point the graph cannot be estimated at all. [Figure 6](#bookmark=id.855hkcuxhk2u) traces this directly: constructibility holds at 100% across the entire forward window for every $N\geq 100$ cell and erodes only at $N=50$, with the earliest and most severe erosion at the steepest ratio. By the final generation only 8 of 20 replicates at the 4:1 ratio still yield a graph (last valid graph at generation 2999), and 3 of 20 at 2.5:1. The failure is gradual rather than a cliff ([Figure 7](#bookmark=id.gcrprvoy337n)): the surviving graphs thin steadily—mean retained edges at $N=50$, 4:1 fall from about 71 near onset to about 71 just before construction fails—so the metrics decay continuously as conditional structure is lost and become undefined only once the covariance is singular. This truncation is the drift floor made visible: the generation at which construction fails is itself a structural readout of the fixation pressure the detection grid characterizes, not a nuisance to be hidden. Every cell remains fully constructible through generation 2744, which defines a common pre-fixation observation horizon. Complete genotype records (200 snapshots per replicate) remain available throughout for ${F}_{ST}$\- and allele-frequency-based analyses.

Net single-snapshot power—counting graph-construction failures as non-detections rather than dropping them—rises with both the migration asymmetry ratio and population size ([Figure 8](#bookmark=id.bsai9oymkk87), [Table 11](#bookmark=id.euylq2rnat4t)). The N \= 50 row is limited not by the index’s sensitivity but by graph construction itself: only 40% of replicates yielded a constructible graph at the 4:1 ratio (the rest having fixed), which caps attainable power there irrespective of signal strength. Among the population sizes that do reach the 80% threshold, the minimum detectable ratio is comparatively stable (2.0–2.5 : 1\) even as the composite scale $4{N}_{e} \Delta m$ at those thresholds spans 6.7–34.3 (a 5.1-fold range). Over this range, then, the detection limit tracks the migration asymmetry *ratio* more closely than the drift-relative composite $4{N}_{e} \Delta m$—consistent with the scale-normalized construction of the asymmetry index, which reads the directional structure of the neighborhood rather than the absolute amount of differentiation drift has produced.

The end-state envelope above reads the grid at the final generation, where the $N=50$ cells are partially truncated due to construction failure. To separate the method’s intrinsic sensitivity from the buildability floor, I re-evaluate the same grid at the common pre-fixation horizon—the latest generation at which *every* cell, $N=50$ included, is still fully constructible ([Figure 6](#bookmark=id.855hkcuxhk2u)). At that horizon, there are no construction failures, so net and conditional power coincide, and every population size is scored on a complete, equally drift-advanced sample.

Evaluated at generation 2744 (about 744 generations after the onset of asymmetric migration), the detection limits are broadly consistent with the end state. At $N=50$, where the end-state row is capped by construction failure, the method reaches the 80% threshold at a 2.5:1 ratio on the complete sample, whereas at the end state it does not reach the threshold at any tested ratio. This places the $N=50$ shortfall at the end state with graph buildability rather than with the index’s sensitivity: given a constructible graph, the directional signal is detectable at small ${N}_{e}$ at a ratio comparable to the larger populations. Either way, separating the two horizons keeps the sensitivity floor (the ratio threshold) distinct from the buildability floor (small\-${N}_{e}$ fixation), which is the cleaner basis for stating the operating envelope.

# Discussion

## **The contribution and its scope**

Genetic gravity converts the undirected skeleton of a Population Graph into a signed field of directional influence using only the conditional edge weights the graph already produces, with no demographic model, no equilibrium assumption, no spatial layer, and no free tuning parameter. The preceding sections establish what the asymmetry index certifies from a single snapshot: it is specific—near zero under symmetric migration however high neutral differentiation rises ([Section 3.5](#bookmark=id.khqknek1q2u2)); its directional content is robust at the network level though bandwidth-sensitive at the level of the individual edge ([Section 3.3](#bookmark=id.8rsolsm8m6z5), [Section 4.3](#bookmark=id.1mlx3kmnkopd)); it has a characterizable detection limit set chiefly by the migration ratio and bounded at small effective size by graph constructibility ([Section 3.7](#bookmark=id.vazmwvg8p772)); and it avoids the drift-driven false positives and fixation artifacts of pairwise differentiation-based estimators ([Section 3.1](#bookmark=id.ebqbpzur3vdc)). The complementary question of how these directional signals *accumulate over time*—the deep-time trajectory of $\Delta ‾$ and the restructuring of the graph’s spectral and path-based metrics under sustained asymmetric connectivity—is the subject of a companion paper.

## **Relationship to existing methods**

The directional methods surveyed in the Introduction each occupy a different point on the trade-off between demographic interpretability and assumption load. Assignment-based estimators of recent migration (Wilson & Rannala, 2003\) and coalescent or site-frequency spectrum approaches (Beerli & Felsenstein, 2001; Gutenkunst et al., 2009\) return demographically interpretable, asymmetric migration rates, but at the cost of equilibrium or explicit-model assumptions, genome-scale data or few populations, and substantial computational cost. Resistance-surface and effective-migration-surface methods (Daniel et al., 2024; Marcus et al., 2021; Petkova et al., 2016\) recover landscape-level effective gene flow, but require spatial coordinates and, in their classical form, treat migration as symmetric. The expansion directionality index $\psi$ (Peter & Slatkin, 2013\) is closest in spirit—reading direction from allele-frequency asymmetry—but is tailored to a single range-expansion origin rather than to an arbitrary connectivity topology. Genetic gravity complements all of these: it provides a topology-derived, coordinate-free index of directional influence that requires no demographic model, no equilibrium assumption, and no spatial layer, can be computed from a single snapshot, and scales trivially with sample size and population size. It is not a replacement for migration-rate estimation; it is a prior structural summary that narrows the hypothesis space for those heavier methods.

The direct comparison with divMigrate ([Section 3.1](#bookmark=id.ebqbpzur3vdc)) makes the trade-off concrete. Where directional flow is genuinely present, the two indices agree on the edges and orientations involved; under symmetric migration, however, divMigrate’s reliance on pairwise differentiation leaves it reporting residual asymmetry driven by drift and effective-size heterogeneity, while the asymmetry index—calibrated against the conditional graph topology—remains near zero. The practical implication is that genetic gravity is the more specific screen for *whether* directionality is present, and the differentiation- and model-based estimators are the appropriate follow-ups for quantifying *how much* and *at what rate*, once it is present.

This contrast is, at root, about *network conditioning*. divMigrate, like any pairwise estimator, evaluates each dyad in isolation: the relative migration assigned to $(i,j)$ is computed from the differentiation of $i$ and $j$ alone, with no reference to the rest of the network. An indirect channel—gene flow from $A$ to $C$ routed through an intermediate $B$—therefore registers as direct $A$–$C$ differentiation, and across a landscape these indirect signals accumulate into a dense field of spurious directional edges that an analyst cannot easily separate from the genuine ones. Genetic gravity begins from a different object. A Population Graph is a Markov random field in which every retained edge has already been conditioned on the rest of the network, and conditionally independent pairs—precisely the indirect $A$–$C$ channels—are pruned before any direction is assigned; the kernel then reads direction only off edges that survived that conditioning. The conditional-independence structure thus acts as a structural filter against false-positive directional edges: the framework cannot manufacture an apparent $A\rightarrow C$ flow that is in fact $A\rightarrow B\rightarrow C$, because the $A$–$C$ edge does not exist in the graph to be directed. This is a categorical rather than incremental advantage—no degree of care in a pairwise method recovers the global conditioning the Population Graph performs by construction—and it is the deeper reason the asymmetry index stays near zero under symmetric migration where a differentiation-based estimator cannot.

The biological interpretation of ${\Delta }_{ij}$ is straightforward when the framework’s assumptions align with the system. Positive ${\Delta }_{ij}$ identifies edges where population $i$ is overwriting the conditional allele-frequency structure of population $j$—a signature of range expansion, source-sink dynamics, or asymmetric vector-mediated dispersal. More broadly, the emergent distinction between “extended” edges (high ${w}_{i\rightarrow j}$ at long ${e}_{ij}$; a putative corridor or expansion front) and “compressed” edges (low ${w}_{i\rightarrow j}$ at short ${e}_{ij}$; a putative vicariance or dispersal barrier) connects the framework directly to the evolutionary processes cataloged in R. Dyer (2007).

## **Scope and reliability: matching the tool to the question**

In the spirit of Box’s dictum that all models are wrong but some are useful, the genetic-gravity kernel is plainly an idealization—it imposes a Gaussian decay and a particular local bandwidth that the biology never guarantees—and the purpose of the simulations was less to vindicate it than to bench-test where it is useful and where it is not. The clearest result to emerge across the validation is a separation by *scale of inference*: the framework is robust at the aggregate, network level, and fragile at the level of the individual edge. The graph-mean asymmetry index $\Delta ‾$ and the longitudinal graph-metric trajectories reliably and specifically recover the presence, mechanism, and timing of asymmetric connectivity (Results; Section 3.5), and the aggregate orientation survives bandwidth changes that reshuffle individual edges ([Section 3.3](#bookmark=id.8rsolsm8m6z5)). Individual-edge orientations, by contrast, are sensitive to the bandwidth choice (roughly one edge in five changes sign under a non-local bandwidth; [Section 3.3](#bookmark=id.8rsolsm8m6z5)), carry a deterministic degree component ([Section 4.4](#bookmark=id.uxcs9kkexnw7)), are not independent observations ([Section 3.6](#bookmark=id.lnhlrjkgu1n)), and are only approximately calibrated by the available per-edge nulls. They are therefore best read as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

This separation organizes the inferential targets into the hierarchy of [Table 1](#bookmark=id.khbia8cnyvgl) and tells a practitioner which tool certifies what. At the network level, the question “is there directional structure, and of what kind?” is answered by $\Delta ‾$ and the graph metrics against the empirical isotropic null, the most reliable use of the framework. The design-driven middle level—“is flow biased along a pre-specified axis?”—is answered by a single one-tailed contrast on the arc weights, which is powerful and unambiguous when sampling is laid out along an expected gradient, provided its significance is referenced to a dependence-respecting null rather than a nominal nonparametric variance ([Section 3.6](#bookmark=id.lnhlrjkgu1n)). Edge-level scanning is the exploratory tier, supported by the fixed-topology permutation (mildly anti-conservative, since its label permutation is a panmixia rather than a symmetric-structure null) or the bandwidth-alignment permutation (conservative, conditioning on the geometry), with multiplicity control. A complementary detection question—“is the signal strong enough relative to drift to be found at all?”—is characterized by the operating envelope ([Section 3.7](#bookmark=id.vazmwvg8p772)), which is governed by the migration ratio and is approximately invariant to ${N}_{e}$ over the tested range, the binding constraint at small ${N}_{e}$ being whether a Population Graph can be constructed at all rather than the index’s sensitivity. And the contrast with divMigrate ([Section 3.1](#bookmark=id.ebqbpzur3vdc)) positions genetic gravity among differentiation-based estimators: concordant where directional flow is genuinely present, more specific under symmetry, and structurally insulated from the fixation-driven normalization blow-up that destabilizes differentiation-magnitude methods ([Section 3.2.1](#bookmark=id.h2b9jyq8vtp8)).

The contribution, then, is not a single index but a suite of complementary tools with an explicit reliability envelope around each. The honest statement of those envelopes—what is robust, what is exploratory, and which null answers which question—is what allows the framework to be applied responsibly: a practitioner who treats $\Delta ‾$ and the graph-level trajectories as load-bearing while reading individual-edge arrows as leads for further work, and who matches the significance test to the sampling design, can extract directional inference from undirected Population Graphs without overreaching what the data support.

## **Caveats and limitations**

Several caveats apply. First, the bandwidth estimator ${b}_{i}={s}_{i}/{k}_{i}$ is a modeling choice, though a constrained one, defended on the three grounds set out in the Methods (parameter-free, scale-equivariant, and singled out by the graph’s Markov structure). A narrower question is whether the *sign* and rank order of ${\Delta }_{ij}$ are preserved under deliberately non-local alternatives—a fixed global bandwidth, a locally rescaled bandwidth, or a perplexity-based per-node bandwidth. [Section 3.3](#bookmark=id.8rsolsm8m6z5) examines this, and the answer is split by scale: the graph-mean $\Delta ‾$ direction is reasonably stable (most so where the directional signal is strong), but individual-edge signs and ranks are only *moderately* preserved—roughly one edge in five changes orientation under a different bandwidth, and rank agreement is weakest under a different kernel. The bandwidth is therefore a genuine modeling choice at the edge level rather than an immaterial convenience; what is robust is the aggregate directional signal, which is what the conclusions rest on, while edge-level ${\Delta }_{ij}$ should be read cautiously (consistent with [Section 3.6](#bookmark=id.lnhlrjkgu1n)). The local estimator remains the principled default on those grounds, not on edge-level invariance.

Second, because the directional weights are normalized within each node’s neighborhood, the asymmetry index carries a purely topological component that is largest at the periphery of the graph. A degree-one node—a chain endpoint, or any population retained with a single neighbor—places its entire kernel mass on that one neighbor, so ${w}_{i\rightarrow j}=1$ irrespective of bandwidth or migration direction; the edge incident to such a node then registers a large $|{\Delta }_{ij}|$ that reflects graph geometry rather than directional gene flow. More generally, whenever two endpoints differ in degree or neighborhood composition, their directional weights differ even under perfectly symmetric migration. This is why $\Delta ‾$ is calibrated against the empirical isotropic null rather than an algebraic zero (Methods), and why edge-level ${\Delta }_{ij}$ values at low-degree nodes should be interpreted cautiously. I examine the boundary contribution directly in [Section 3.4](#bookmark=id.ey67elsdfoir), decomposing the signal into leaf-incident and interior edges and showing that the scenario discrimination is an interior property while the termini mark a genuinely drift-dominated evolutionary regime; a principled correction—for instance, conditioning the null on node degree—is a natural further refinement I leave to future work.

Third, the local kernel only reads directionality off edges that already exist in the topology; populations that are conditionally independent in the Population Graph receive zero directional weight by construction. The method therefore inherits the edge-retention properties of the underlying Population Graph estimator, and directional inference is only as good as the topology it rides on. Fourth, ${\Delta }_{ij}$ reflects the *structural* influence of one population on another, not a demographic migration rate; inferring $m$ from $\Delta$ requires an additional model and is outside the scope of this work. Finally, the validation simulations omit mutation, so allelic diversity decays slowly under drift across the 3,000-generation horizon; this isolates the drift–migration processes the framework is designed to detect, but it bounds the window over which the metapopulation retains the polymorphism needed to estimate conditional covariance, and a mutation–drift treatment would be required to extend the analysis to longer timescales.

## **Extensions**

Several extensions follow naturally. Landscape-conditioned genetic gravity—in which the kernel bandwidth ${b}_{i}$ is modulated by at-site ecological covariates, after the gravity-model treatment of pollen flow in Dileo et al. (2014)—should improve the biological specificity of the directional signal. Temporal sampling, either by resampling the same populations across years or by using time-structured genotypes, converts the static ${\Delta }_{ij}$ into a series whose derivative directly encodes the expansion rate. And the fixed-topology permutation framework introduced above generalizes to multi-edge test statistics (e.g., mean $|\Delta |$ over all edges incident to a node) that support hypothesis tests about individual populations acting as sources or sinks.  
Taken together, these results argue that directionality is recoverable from the information already present in a Population Graph, without introducing new modeling machinery, and that the asymmetry index ${\Delta }_{ij}$—together with the directed-graph metrics and $\Delta ‾$ it generates—provides a useful, interpretable summary of that directional information. The approach is implemented in the gstudio R package and is ready for use on existing data.

The asymmetry index, the local-bandwidth estimator, and the significance procedures described here are implemented in the open-source gstudio R package (R. ## **Appendix**

### **Notation**

Briefly, $K$ populations are represented as centroids in a multivariate genetic space, with the $K\times K$ Euclidean distance matrix $D$ double-centered via the idempotent operator $H=I-\frac{1}{K}1{1}^{T}$ to yield the covariance matrix $C=-\frac{1}{2}H{D}^{2}H$ (Dyer et al., 2004; Gower, 1966). Population Graph edges are retained where the corresponding partial correlation (from ${C}^{-1}$) is significant, so that an edge ${e}_{ij}\in E$ indicates a conditional genetic dependency between $i$ and $j$ that cannot be explained through intermediate populations (Dyer & Nason, 2004). I write $N(i)$ for the neighbors of node $i$ in $G$ and ${k}_{i}=|N(i)|$ for its degree.

### **Bandwidth is Not Degree**

This makes the natural objection—that ${\Delta }_{ij}$ is merely a function of node degree and neighborhood composition—correct only in form but not in consequence. The parameter ${\Delta }_{ij}$ is indeed a deterministic function of the graph; it has no other inputs. The question is whether the neighborhood scales ${b}_{i}$ ${b}_{j}$ carry *biological* directionality or only *topological* position, and whether the two contributions are separable. Topology contributes a component that is present even under symmetric migration and is largest at low-degree boundary nodes (the leaf-node effect discussed below); this component is independent of the direction of gene flow. Directional gene flow contributes a second component through its effect on neighborhood scale: when population $i$ persistently exports migrants to population $j$, it homogenizes $j$’s allele frequencies toward its own, compressing the conditional distances in $j$’s neighborhood (shrinking ${b}_{j}$) while $i$’s own neighborhood scale is comparatively preserved. The resulting ${b}_{i}>{b}_{j}$ is exactly the configuration that drives ${\Delta }_{ij}>0$, so the bandwidth difference is a reading of *introgression pressure* rather than of centrality alone. Crucially, the two components are distinguished empirically rather than by assertion: because every scenario branches from a shared burn-in state with the same stepping-stone connectivity, the purely topological contribution is shared with the isotropic null and cannot separate the scenarios. The validation bears this out—$\Delta ‾\approx 0$ and single-snapshot discrimination is at random under symmetric migration, with both rising only once migration becomes asymmetric, as demonstrated by the simulation results show. A degree artifact cannot discriminate between scenarios that share a topology; the observed separation is therefore migration-driven, not the sole property of the graph’s pattern of connectivity.

This separation can be made explicit. In the *no-direction limit*, where node $i$’s retained neighbor distances are equal, the directional weight reduces to the uniform value ${w}_{i\rightarrow j}=1/{k}_{i}$ and the asymmetry index collapses to a purely topological term that depends only on the endpoint degrees,

$${\Delta }_{ij}^{0} = \frac{1}{{k}_{i}}-\frac{1}{{k}_{j}} = \frac{{k}_{j}-{k}_{i}}{{k}_{i} {k}_{j}},$$					\[A1\]

which vanishes exactly when the endpoints share a degree, grows with the degree imbalance, and is largest at low-degree nodes—recovering a leaf-node effect (where ${k}_{i}=1$ forces ${w}_{i\rightarrow j}=1$) as the extreme case. Writing ${\Delta }_{ij}={\Delta }_{ij}^{0}+{\delta }_{ij}$, the directional signal is carried entirely by the second term ${\delta }_{ij}$, which enters only through the bandwidth contrast ${b}_{i}\ne {b}_{j}$ that asymmetric gene flow induces; ${\Delta }_{ij}^{0}$ is invariant to migration. Because every forward scenario branches from a shared burn-in topology, the topological term ${\Delta }_{ij}^{0}$ is held in common with the isotropic null and cancels in any scenario contrast—which is precisely why $\Delta ‾$ is calibrated against the empirical isotropic null rather than an algebraic zero ([Equation ](#bookmark=id.eq2w2pieu1oh)A1 makes the term being absorbed explicit).
