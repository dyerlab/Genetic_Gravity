# Abstract

Winds, currents, river networks, source–sink gradients and range expansions all bias the direction of gene flow, yet most summaries of genetic connectivity are symmetric. Here I introduce an extension of Population Graphs, genetic gravity, that reads direction from conditional covariance without requiring underlying demographic models or equilibrium assumptions. Eedges are evaluated based on connected population neighborhood importance, and resulting asymmetries are summarized as a population-level genetic source–sink scores. I demonstrate two tests for asymmetry: a comparison of directional and symmetric isolation by graph distance, and a gradient test for evaluating *a priori* hypotheses. Individual-based simulations showed revealed nominal false-positive rates (2.9–4.2% at α = 0.05) across a ten-fold range of migration, and directionality of asymmetry was recovered reliably. Against the true migration matrix, the neighborhood weights separated migration corridors more cleanly than the structure-based `divMigrate` model. Using the Sonoran Desert bark beetle *Araptus attenuatus* as a case study, genetic gravtity identified southward post-Pleistocene expansion suggested by diversity gradients that symmetric isolation by distance missed. Genetic gravity offers a lightweight, testable summary of directional connectivity suitable for landscape and population genetic analyses.

*Keywords:* asymmetric gene flow, conditional genetic distance, landscape genetics, Population Graphs, source-sink dynamics, range expansion.

# Introduction

Inferring the pattern and direction of genetic connectivity from molecular data is a central problem in population and landscape genetics (Slatkin, 1985; Dyer, 2015). Many of the processes that structure natural populations are directional: prevailing winds and ocean currents, the downstream bias of river networks, gradients in habitat quality that create source–sink dynamics, and range expansions out of refugia all bias the realized direction of dispersal (Pringle et al., 2011; Paz-Vinas & Blanchet, 2015; Paz-Vinas et al., 2015; Baker & Dyer, 2011; Wogan et al., 2024). Yet the summaries most often used to describe connectivity treat it as symmetric. Structure statistics such as $F_{ST}$ (and its analogs) return a symmetric value for each pair of populations, $F_{ST(i,j)}=F_{ST(j,i)}$, and under an island model ($F_{ST}\approx1/(4N_em+1)$; Wright, 1931) reflects the combination of genetic effective size and migration rather than the direction of exchange (Whitlock & McCauley, 1999). However, similar magnitudes of structure can arise from very different histories, and a scalar, undirected summary averages over exactly the heterogeneity that questions about colonization, source–sink dynamics and directional dispersal seek to ask.

We are not without tools for these questions. Assignment-based methods such as `BayesAss` infer recent, directional migration rates from multilocus genotypes (Wilson & Rannala, 2003) and coalescent and isolation-with-migration frameworks recover asymmetric long-term migration matrices using reconstructed genealogies (Beerli & Felsenstein, 2001) or site-frequency spectra of a few populations (Gutenkunst et al., 2009). If directionality arises from range expansion, we may infer directionality from gradients in genetic diversity (e.g., Garrick et al., 2013), derive a directionality index from pairwise allele-frequency asymmetries (Peter & Slatkin, 2013), or construct landscape scale effective-migration-surface maps (Petkova et al., 2016; Marcus et al., 2021). Each is powerful within its own domain, but each also carries a characteristic cost: a prespecified demographic or spatial model, an assumption of migration–drift equilibrium, genome-scale data, few populations, explicit spatial coordinates, or substantial computation. Differentiation-based estimators such as `divMigrate` (Sundqvist et al., 2016) are lighter, but read direction one pair of populations at a time. Symmetric summaries may also mislead as directional gene flow can obscure isolation by distance, which is interpreted as weak spatial structure with high connectivity.

Population Graphs provide an alternative perspective on spatial genetic structure (Dyer & Nason, 2004; Dyer, 2007, 2015). Using conditional genetic covariance, a Population Graph translates genetic variance into a graph-theoretic network: each edge is estimated from the joint multilocus covariance among all populations, shifting the focus from a single index to focus on the network's topological shape. Neutral demographic processes, including range expansion, vicariance, and isolation by distance, each leave characteristic topological features (Dyer, 2007, 2015). Population Graphs have found applications in landscape genetics (Dyer, 2015; Daniel et al., 2024), conservation planning (Vilaça et al., 2024), biogeography (Wogan et al., 2024), life-history comparisons (Reed et al., 2023), invasion biology (Baker & Dyer, 2011), and pollen-mediated gene flow (DiLeo et al., 2014).  However, graph edges are undirected and based on population covariance, which may arise from either symmetric exchange or a one-way colonization pulses.

Here, I introduce the concept of *genetic gravity*, an extension to Population Graphs that extracts directional information from its otherwise undirected topology, based only on graph structure itself. Despite the shared name, it is distinct from the gravity models used in landscape genetics (Murphy et al., 2010; DiLeo et al., 2014), which regress genetic connectivity between sites on at-site and between-site covariates; genetic gravity uses no covariates and is read from the Population Graph alone.  Edge level asymmetry is defined on Markov dependence through a node-local Gaussian kernel.  I provide two complementary statistical tests for asymmetry based on standing structure and spatial isolation.  Performance is evaluated against inference using `divMigrate` and apply the framework to published case study of a Sonoran Desert bark beetle whose phylogeographic structure predicted a complex post-Pleistocene expansion process (Garrick et al., 2013).  All methods are implemented in the R package `gstudio`.

# Methods

The framework assumes that a Population Graph has already been constructed from multilocus genotypes; its inputs are the symmetric edge weights $e_{ij}$ and the adjacency structure of the graph $G=\{V,E\}$, with notation following Dyer & Nason (2004) and Dyer (2007; Supplemental, S1, Table S1). A Population Graph is a Markov independence graph, and two of its properties matter here. First, populations joined by an edge are *conditionally dependent*, through ongoing gene flow, recent shared ancestry, or some combination thereof. Populations joined only through others ($A\leftrightarrow B\leftrightarrow C$) are *conditionally independent* given the intermediates. An edge therefore represents a direct relationship between two populations given their covariance with the entire data set. 

## The Model of Genetic Gravity

Each symmetric edge in a Population Graph is the pooled product of two conditional relationships: the contribution of population $j$ to the genetic structure of $i$, and the opposite contribution of $i$ to $j$. The edge weight $e_{ij}$ is the conditional genetic distance (cGD; Dyer et al. 2010) used to infer isolation by graph distance (Dyer, 2015; Miles et al., 2018). Focusing on each population and its retained neighbors, $N(i)$, I define a *neighborhood weight*, $w_{j\mid i}$, as $j$'s weight in $i$'s neighborhood, following Gaussian stochastic neighbor embedding (SNE; Hinton & Roweis, 2003):

$$
w_{j\mid i}= \frac{\exp\left(- e_{ij}^{2}/2b_i^{2}\right)}{\sum\limits_{k\in N(i)}\exp\left(- e_{ik}^{2}/2b_i^{2}\right)}
$$

The kernel is local, populations that are conditionally independent (e.g., not directly connected) of $i$ contribute nothing. The node-specific *local bandwidth* $b_i$ sets how quickly the kernel decays with edge distance, in the neighborhood of node $i$,

$$
b_{i} = \gamma\,\frac{s_{i}}{k_{i}} = \frac{\gamma}{k_{i}}\sum\limits_{j\in N(i)}e_{ij},
$$

where $s_i$ is the strength (weighted degree) of population $i$, $k_i$ its degree, and $\gamma$ a fixed multiplier: setting $\gamma=\tfrac{1}{2}$ to infer the direction of gene flow, whereas a $\gamma=1$, describes a population's overall weight in the network (Figure 1; below, see Supplemental Materials S8 for additional information).

**Genetic gravity.** The weight of each edge is read from both ends, $w_{j\mid i}$ from within $i$'s neighborhood and $w_{i\mid j}$ from $j$’s.  As each nodes neighborhood is different, the two weights for each edge also differ. The difference in weights on an edge defines the *gravity asymmetry* for that edge,
$$
\Delta_{i\rightarrow j} = w_{i\mid j}-w_{j\mid i},
$$
which is interpreted as node $i$'s pull on $j$ minus node $j$'s pull on $i$. The asymmetry comes not from the edge itself but from the two nodes neighborhoods: the same $e_{ij}$ can be typical of $i$'s neighborhood and atypical for $j$'s. A value near zero indicates balanced exchange (symmetry); a positive value marks $i$ as the genetic source and $j$ as the sink (and vice-versa). Under this construct, higher values point towards a *source* and edges that share a population are not independent, so direction can now be summarized at the population level. 

The *gravity* of population $i$ can be defined as:
$$
g_{i} = \sum\limits_{j\in N(i)} w_{i\mid j},
$$
and defines the total weight $i$ holds in its neighbors' neighborhood. Because each population's own weights sum to one, gravity is conserved, $\sum_i g_i=K$, and the source–sink balance $g_i-1$ sums to zero over the entire graph: every population that reads as a source is balanced by populations that read as sinks. 

**Source–sink scores.** While gravity summarizes each population separately, the *source–sink score*, $S$, places all populations on a single scale. The source-sink score, $S$, assigns each population the value whose pairwise differences best reproduce the gravity asymmetries across all edges,
$$
\hat{S}=\arg\min_{S}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\left[\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}-(S_i-S_j)\right]^2, \qquad \sum_{i}S_i=0 \text{ within each connected component},
$$
such that $S_i-S_j\approx\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$. A node with a high $S$ marks a source whereas one with a low $S$ represents a sink. Gravity and the source–sink scores are two views of the same quantity. Because $\Delta_{j\rightarrow i}=-\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$ and each population's own neighborhood weights sum to one, the gravity balance is the *net asymmetry* over a population's edges, $g_i-1=\sum_{j\in N(i)}\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$.  

In practice the scores are estimated by least squares, solving $L\hat{S}=g-1$, where $L$ is the Laplacian of the graph (Supplemental, S2.4). The score assembles these local imbalances into a single surface, much as elevations are recovered from height differences between neighboring points: populations high on this surface are those whose genetic structure has spread to their neighbors (an upstream deme, the core of a range expansion), and populations low on it are those that have received it. The scores can be displayed as a *source–sink surface* (Supplemental, S2.6), where $S$ is smoothed and visualized in either graph space (e.g., Dyer & Nason, 2004; Figure 1) or in geographic space (as below). Drawing each edge as an arrow scaled by $\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$ gives a *gravity-field plot*. 

### Directional Conditional Genetic Distance, dcGD

The same two weights also split each conditional genetic distance (cGD; Dyer et al. 2010) into directional components. The *directional conditional genetic distance* (dcGD) is $p_{ij} = e_{ij}\,w_{j\mid i}/(w_{j\mid i}+w_{i\mid j})$, with $p_{ji}$ defined as $p_{ij}+p_{ji}=e_{ij}$ and $p_{ij}=p_{ji}=e_{ij}/2$ when the two weights are equal. Because dcGD is a distance, it reads opposite to the source–sink convention: conditional distance is shorter in the direction of gene flow. Using dcGD turns the undirected Population Graph into a directed, asymmetrically weighted graph over the same topology, which can replace cGD in the analyses of isolation by graph distance (IBGD; Dyer & Nason, 2004), ecological resistance modeling (Dyer, 2015), studies of range expansion (Baker & Dyer, 2011), etc.

## Individual-based simulations

The behavior of genetic gravity was evaluated with individual-based stochastic simulations following Dyer (2007). Each replicate tracks a metapopulation of $K=25$ demes of $N=100$ diploid individuals scored at $L=20$ independently segregating, codominant, biallelic loci. Replicates start from random allele frequencies; generations are non-overlapping, mating is random within demes, and mutation is excluded so that only migration and drift act. Every replicate first runs a 2,000-generation *burn-in* of symmetric migration along a one-dimensional stepping stone with a fixed per-direction migration rate ($m_{i\rightarrow i+1}=m_{i\leftarrow i+1}=0.025$). Fifty replicates were run, each producing an independent *burn-in* history (hereafter a *lineage*). Multilocus genotypes were saved every five generations, and each saved snapshot (hereafter a *census*) was converted into a Population Graph, from which the neighborhood weights, gravity asymmetries and source–sink scores were computed. Mean degree centrality ($\bar{C}_D$,) and graph diameter ($D_G$; graph-theoretic surrogates for $F_{ST}$ and $\hat{M}$) were also recorded for comparison to Dyer (2007).

At generation 2000, each lineage was continued for an addition 1,000 generations (generations 2000–2999, hereafter referred to as the *forward phase*) under each of three different migration scenarios (Table 1) so that all three scenarios continue the same lineage and differ only in the migration matrix applied from generation 2000. *Symmetric* migration continued the *burn-in* matrix and serves as the null trajectory. *Redistributed* migration kept the total rate fixed but split it 4:1 in one direction along the stepping stone model, which maintained the overall migration rate identical to the symmetric case. An *Obstructed* migration scenario kept the *Symmetric* forward rate but suppressed the reverse rate. Three further symmetric scenarios at progressively lower migration rates—sym-mid, sym-low and sym-verylow (0.010, 0.005 and 0.0025 in each direction, 0.4, 0.2 and 0.1 times the *Symmetric* rate; Table 1)—raise neutral differentiation without imposing a direction to explore a *drift gradient* used to calibrate false-positive rates (Supplemental, S7.1).

### Phases of the forward simulations

Changing a migration matrix may initiate distinct hases in structure depending upon a lineages history, the distribution of genetic diversity, and the imposed scenario.  I define the following phases for simulated data sets. The *ascent* begins with a change in migration and restructures among-population covariance; upstream demes receive fewer immigrants and lose heterozygosity faster.  I define the end of *ascent* as the generation when the gradient in within-deme $H_e$ has completed 90% of its rise (used only to date the boundary, not for direction). Next, the *plateau* is defined by the period of time under stable covariance during which allele frequencies converge due to connectivity. Some lineages then enter a *decay* phase where among-population variance is lost due to drift and homogenization.  I set the start of *decay* as an *informative horizon*, beyond which demes lose statistical genetic inferences, and chose a cutoff when average population polymorphism goes below 4.5 loci (many demes are fixed at this point).  I use these boundaries only as heuristics for separating demographic regimes within each lineage (and not based on graph structure), not constants on the system. In the summaries that follow, measurements are taken from each lineage's *phase core* (the middle generation of each phase).  Sensitivity to phase boundaries is discussed in Supplemental S3.3.  
## Significance Testing for Asymmetry

I explored significance testing via both permutation and resampling and found them to be anti-conservative under symmetric migration scenarios (see Supplemental S5.4 for a more detailed discussion), so instead evaluated asymmetry in a gradient test on the source–sink score, and by a comparison of directional (dcGD) and symmetric (cGD) isolation-by-graph-distance models. 

**Source–sink gradient test.** This test asks whether the source–sink scores change monotonically along an axis, such as physical position along a hypothesized direction of flow. The statistic is Spearman's $r(S,x)$; because high $S$ marks a source, $r(S,x)<0$ places the sources at low values of $x$. The null preserves the spatial structure of $S$ on the census's own Population Graph, using a Moran spectral randomization (Wagner & Dray, 2015) on the kernel-weighted adjacency with $B=999$ randomizations, it is tested as a two-tailed test (see Supplemental, S5.1). Because it reproduces the smoothness that $S$ inherits from genetic drift on the graph, a rejection reflects a gradient along the covariate beyond what drift alone produces.

**Directional isolation by graph distance.** This test asks whether symmetric or asymmetric covariance creates a better fit to models of isolation (graph) by distance (Wright, 1943). Standard IBGD regresses all-pairs cGD (shortest paths over $e_{ij}$) on pairwise spatial separation $|dx_{ij}|$, giving $R^2_{cGD}$. The directional model uses all-pairs directed shortest paths over dcGD, so that the distance from $i$ to $j$ may differ from that from $j$ to $i$, and a hypothesized axis of flow (in the simulations here, the stepping stone configuration) classifies each ordered pair as forward or reverse; $\text{dcGD}_{ij} \sim |dx_{ij}| + |dx_{ij}| \cdot r_{ij}$, with $r_{ij}=1$ for reverse pairs, then gives separate forward and reverse isolation slopes and a fit $R^2_{dcGD}$ adjusted for the additional slope. The improvement, $\Delta R^2 = R^2_{dcGD} - R^2_{cGD}$, is judged against calibrated test that simulates symmetric connectivity using the same spatial-genetic configuration as the real data.  Where the directional model provides a better fit, the ratio of forward to reverse slopes, $b_{fwd}/b_{rev}$, describes the direction of isolation.  Because the two fits use different response matrices, the comparison is not a nested-model test, and its error rates must be characterized empirically.

## Bandwidth

The bandwidth sets the scale at which a population's neighbors are compared, and with it how much of $\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$ reflects genetic distance and how much the number of neighbors. As the kernel flattens, every weight tends to $1/k_i$, so the index reduces to a *topological floor*, $\Delta^{0}_{i\rightarrow j}=1/k_j-1/k_i$, and the source–sink score to $S^{0}_i=-1/k_i$, both set by degree alone. When the question is the direction of gene flow, degree is a nuisance; when it is a population's overall weight in the network, degree is part of the answer. Two bandwidths are therefore offered. The *degree-neutral* bandwidth, $\gamma=\tfrac{1}{2}$, is the multiplier at which $S$ carries no degree signature under symmetric migration (median 0.49 across *Symmetric* censuses, and 0.51 in a second, independently simulated set of 50 lineages). It is fixed once from the symmetric null rather than tuned on each graph, because tuning lets the signal under test move the bandwidth (Supplemental, S8.2), and it is used for both tests and for the source–sink surface. The *local mean*, $\gamma=1$, keeps the contribution of degree, so that hubs read as sources and leaves as sinks; it describes a population's weight in the network but is not suited to testing direction. With either bandwidth, the *degree share*—the $R^2$ of $\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$ on $\Delta^{0}_{i\rightarrow j}$—reports how much of the index degree sets (Figure 1). Sensitivity to alternative bandwidths is assessed in Supplemental, S8.1.

## Sampling intensity and design-specific calibration

The sampling design affects the two tests differently. The source–sink gradient test draws its null from the study's own graph, so its false-positive rate stays near $\alpha$ whatever the design; the design changes only its power. The directional isolation comparison has no such null: the rule $\Delta R^2>0$ asks only whether separate forward and reverse slopes improve the fit, and in small or thinly sampled graphs they often do by chance. For a given design, we can calibrate by simulating data sets under symmetric migration with the study's own sites, sample sizes, and loci, and with migration tuned to the observed differentiation. The 95th percentile of $\Delta R^2$ across these data sets define the cutoff, and the fraction at or above the observed value is a one-sided $p$-value. The same data sets also give a design-matched null for the source–sink test. 

The tests are interpreted as follows. If $\Delta R^2$ exceeds the calibrated cutoff, the directional model explains isolation better than symmetric migration would for that specific design, and $b_{fwd}/b_{rev}$ gives the direction. If $\Delta R^2$ is positive but below the cutoff, the improvement is no larger than symmetric migration produces by chance; if it is negative, the symmetric model suffices. Neither case is evidence of symmetric migration. A source–sink test that does not reject is weighed against its power, which falls steeply with the number of populations. A large degree share, or a fragmented graph, means the scores largely reflect how many neighbors each population has. Comparing the observed degree share with the design-matched null distinguishes two causes: if it is typical of the null, thin sampling explains it; if it exceeds the null, the graph's degree structure itself departs from symmetric exchange. Either way, the calibrated directional isolation comparison is the more reliable test.

To evaluate these effects, I rebuilt Population Graphs from subsets of the simulated data: 25, 21, 18, 15, 12, 10, 8 or 5 demes spread along the stepping stone, with 100, 50, 30, 20 or 10 individuals per deme and, for a subset of designs, fewer loci (10 and 8 rather than 20 used; Supplemental, S10). Each test was applied to *plateau* phase-core data sets, for power, and to *Symmetric* and sym-low censuses, for false positives. Cutoffs for each design were calibrated on half of the *Symmetric* lineages and evaluated on the other half.

## Comparison with a differentiation-based method

To place genetic gravity alongside an existing method, simulated data were also analyzed with `divMigrate` (Sundqvist et al., 2016), a widely used estimator of directional relative migration from codominant genotypes. The two do not estimate the same quantity—`divMigrate` reads relative migration from pairwise differentiation, using the $Nm$ statistic of Alcalá et al. (2014), whereas gravity is read from conditional genetic structure—so the comparison is not between the two methods but the extent to which each recapitulates the underlying, simulation defined, migration matrix.  Agreement was summarized for each approach across all census data sets using Spearman's $\rho$. 

## Case Study: Sonoran Desert Bark Beetle

To show how the framework handles specific *a priori* hypotheses of direction, I applied it to the Sonoran Desert bark beetle *Araptus attenuatus* (Curculionidae). Garrick et al. (2013) found a widespread peninsular clade whose latitudinal gradients in nuclear allelic richness indicated a complex expansion process, both northward and southward, out of a Pleistocene refugium near 27.5°N. I reanalyzed the nuclear genotypes of that clade including 240 individuals from 27 sites between 23.3°N and 29.3°N.  All animals were genotyped at eight codominant nuclear loci (seven intron-spanning gene regions and one anonymous microsatellite; Garrick et al., 2013). Each expansion was tested on the Population Graph under the hypothesis that populations near the refugium should read as sources and those farther from it as sinks, and genetic distance should accumulate more slowly moving away from the refugium than toward it. Distance from the refugium, $d_i$, was measured along the long axis of the peninsula. For the directional isolation comparison, separation was $|d_i-d_j|$, a pair was forward when $j$ lies farther from the refugium than $i$, and the hypothesis predicts $\Delta R^2$ above the calibrated cutoff with $b_{fwd}/b_{rev}<1$; standard isolation by graph distance was assessed with a Mantel test (999 permutations). All quantities were computed at the degree-neutral bandwidth, with the degree share reported alongside, and the source–sink surface was drawn in geographic space. Both tests were also judged against a null calibrated for this design as described above, from 999 data sets simulated with the observed sites, sample sizes and loci and with migration tuned to the observed differentiation ($G_{ST}=0.14$; Supplemental, S10.6).

# Results

## *Symmetric* baseline

Under *Symmetric* migration, the Population Graphs reached a stable topology within the 2,000-generation *burn-in*: mean degree centrality fell and graph diameter rose toward a plateau (as in Dyer 2007) and stayed flat through the forward phase (Supplemental, S3.5, Figure S2). The mean asymmetry stayed close to zero ($\bar{\Delta}=-0.0015$) over the forward phase. Averaged within lineages, the source–sink score showed no consistent directional bias beyond ordinary between-lineage variation (Supplemental, S7.1).  At the local mean ($\gamma=1$), $S$ tracked the degree-only score $S^{0}$ under symmetric migration (median correlation 0.78–0.83), so hubs read as sources and leaves as sinks; at the degree-neutral bandwidth it did not (0.01–0.06). Degree-one leaves, sinks by construction, were rare and barely affected the source–sink gradient (see Supplemental, S3.5).

## Consequences of *Redistributed* and *Obstructed* connectivity

Every *Redistributed* lineage passed through all three phases (*ascent*, *plateau*, and *decay*) within the forward phase (Figure 2, left) with the timing of, and residence time within, each phase unique to each lineage. *Ascent* ended at a median of generation 2332, the *plateau* lasted a median of 325 generations, and *decay* began at a median of generation 2674. Across these phases mean degree centrality fell ($\bar{C}_D =$ 0.309, 0.274 and 0.236) compared to the *Symmetric* scenario ($\bar{C}_D =$ 0.315) and diameter, after rising slightly near the end of *ascent*, collapsed ($D_G =$ 15.8 on the *plateau* and 10.2 after the horizon) compared to the *Symmetric* scenario ($D_G =$ 16.3), while mean $|\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}|$ rose (0.049, 0.080 and 0.147). Sustained directional bias in a closed system thus both makes gene flow asymmetric and progressively homogenizes the metapopulation.  Part of the rise after the onset of the *decay* phase is information loss rather than signal due to genetic homogeneity (Supplemental, S3.4, Figure S1). The signed mean $\bar{\Delta}$ was positive in 98% of lineages during *ascent* and on the *plateau*, but negative in 70% after the initiation of the *decay* phase. This sign change is not a reversal of gene flow: it is carried by rewiring of the graph's interior, and the source–sink score kept the imposed ordering through it (see below and Supplemental, S4.3).

*Obstructed* migration, with half the net directional bias in migration rates, passed through the same phases, though more slowly (Figure 2, right). *Obstructed* lineages reached each stage 1.8 to 2.8 times later than *Redistributed* ones (1.9 times later for the end of *ascent*), close to the two-fold difference in net bias. Diameter rose further and held longer before declining, as lower total migration let drift differentiate the metapopulation further before directional homogenization took over. Once on the *plateau*, however, *Obstructed* lineages resembled *Redistributed* ones (mean degree centrality 0.270 against 0.274, mean $|\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}|$ 0.074 against 0.080, $\bar{\Delta}$ 0.030 against 0.034): the net bias set how quickly a lineage reached its *plateau*, not how strong the directional signal was once there. Only 6 of the 50 *Obstructed* lineages crossed the informative horizon into the *decay* phase, so *Obstructed* results are reported for *ascent* and the *plateau* only.

## Testing for Asymmetry

**Source–sink gradient test.** Under symmetric migration the source–sink gradient test held its nominal rate (Table 2). At $\alpha=0.05$ its false-positive rate was 3.0% in *burn-in* censuses and 2.9–4.2% in forward-phase censuses across the drift gradient at lower migration rates, down to 0.25 migrants per deme per generation in each direction, with no increase as neutral differentiation; lineage-clustered upper limits were 5.8% or less.

Under asymmetric migration, single-census power tracked the phases (Figure 3, bottom; Supplemental, S5.3, Table S6). In each phase core the rejection rate under *Redistributed* migration was 15% during *ascent*, 65% on the *plateau* and 26% after the informative horizon, and under *Obstructed* migration 10% during *ascent* and 45% on the *plateau*. The direction was far more consistent than any single census could certify: on the *plateau*, $r(S,x)$ was negative—sources upstream—in 98% and 99% of censuses (median $-0.81$ and $-0.73$), and every lineage placed the sources upstream when its *plateau* censuses were averaged, against 38% of *Symmetric* lineages. Even after entering the *decay* phase, *Redistributed* lineages still placed the sources upstream in 86% of censuses.

The deme-by-generation display of $S$ shows where the gradient comes from (Figure 3, top). Under symmetric migration the interior is close to neutral, with a mild sink at each terminal deme. Once asymmetry is imposed, the downstream end deepens into a sink within 200–400 generations and a band of sources forms upstream, centered on deme 3 in both scenarios. Under *Obstructed* migration the band holds its place; under *Redistributed* migration it moves downstream after about generation 2600, to deme 11 by the end of the window, while the demes it leaves behind fade to neutral, so the collapse of a lineage advances downstream from its upstream end. Single-census gravity-field plots of each state are shown in Supplemental, S4.4, Figure S4.

**Directional isolation by graph distance.** Standard isolation by graph distance weakened once migration became asymmetric (Figure 4, top; Table 3). The $R^2$ of cGD on spatial separation stayed near 0.80 under *Symmetric* migration but fell under *Redistributed* migration from 0.78 during *ascent* to 0.63 on the *plateau* and 0.38 after the horizon, and under *Obstructed* migration to 0.68 on the *plateau*. Part of what is lost is directional structure that the direction-aware dcGD model recovers: on the *plateau* its fit exceeded the standard symmetric fit (0.74 against 0.63 under *Redistributed* and 0.75 against 0.68 under *Obstructed* migration), whereas under *Symmetric* migration it fell well short (0.53 against 0.80). The directional model therefore improves on estimates of Isolation By Graph Distance when, and largely only when, gene flow is asymmetric; for ordinary isolation by distance, cGD remains the appropriate distance (Supplemental, S6.2).

Under asymmetric migration the directional model's advantage tracked the phases (Table 3, last column): it was preferred in 26% and 23% of *ascent* censuses under *Redistributed* and *Obstructed* migration, 89% and 78% on the *plateau*, and 82% of *Redistributed* censuses after the horizon. Every detection in the phase cores recovered the imposed direction ($b_{fwd}/b_{rev}<1$; *plateau* medians 0.06 and 0.08), against a median ratio of 1.26 over all *Symmetric* censuses. The test is one-sided: a preferred symmetric fit is not evidence against asymmetry, since even on the *plateau* 11% of *Redistributed* and 22% of *Obstructed* censuses favored cGD.

Under symmetric migration the decision rule $\Delta R^2>0$ rarely fired: in 2.6% of *burn-in* censuses (lineage-clustered 95% CI 1.0–4.6%) and 4.4% across the *Symmetric* forward phase (2.8–6.1%). It was not calibrated once drift dominated (i.e., at very low migration rates): across the drift gradient the rate rose from 6.9% (sym-mid; 1.0 migrant per deme per generation in each direction) to 12.2% (sym-low; 0.5) and 14.6% (sym-verylow; 0.25), with no consistent direction along the axis (Supplemental, S6.4, Table S7; Section S7.1).

### Bandwidth sensitivity

Scaling the bandwidth parameter, $\gamma$, from $\gamma=0.25$ to 2 showed how much of the asymmetry index reflects the number of neighbors each population has (its degree) rather than genetic distance: under symmetric migration it explained 2–4% of the variance in $\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$ at $\gamma\le\tfrac{1}{2}$, 50% at the local mean and 94% at $\gamma=2$ (Supplemental, S8.1, Figure S5). As a check, I independently simulated 50 additional lineages and evaluated the degree-neutral bandwidth, which again held its low false-positive rate and outperformed the local mean on both tests (Supplemental, S8.1, Table S12). Tuning the multiplier separately for each graph, or using kernels that do not scale with each population's own neighborhood, were found to generally lose statistical power (Supplementary Materials, Sections S8.1–S8.2). Edge-level orientation was the least stable quantity: between $\gamma=1$ and $\tfrac{1}{2}$, 22–28% of edges changed sign, yet every lineage placed the sources upstream on the *plateau* at both bandwidths, and 95–100% did so at every value of $\gamma$ from 0.25 to 1.41.

### Sampling intensity

Sampling intensity determined which test could establish direction (Supplemental, S10, Figure S6, Table S13). The number of populations set the power of the source–sink test, which remained calibrated throughout: with 30 individuals per population, *plateau* power under *Redistributed* migration fell from 0.70 with 25 populations to 0.37 with 15 and 0.04 with 8. The rule $\Delta R^2>0$ became anti-conservative as populations were removed, firing in 9% of *Symmetric* graphs with 15 populations, 14% with 10 and 41% with 5, but a cutoff calibrated for each combination of populations and individuals restored a 5% false-positive rate on held-out lineages and kept more power than the source–sink test (0.68 with 15 populations and 0.53 with 10). That cutoff did not protect against patterns created by genetic drift, under which it fired in 7–13% of graphs. The number of individuals per population governed whether the graph held together: with 10 per population about half of the graphs were fragmented and the degree-neutral bandwidth retained a degree signature, whereas with 30 at most 11% were fragmented. Reducing the loci from 20 to 8 cut source–sink power at 25 populations from 0.70 to 0.49, and sampling a contiguous block rather than the whole axis cut it further. Under asymmetric migration the sign of $r(S,x)$ still placed the sources upstream in 89–99% of graphs with as few as five populations, against about 40% under *Symmetric* migration.

## Comparison with `divMigrate`

Against the true migration matrix, the neighborhood weight $w_{j\mid i}$ agreed more closely than `divMigrate`'s $Nm$ (Figure 5): median Spearman's $\rho$ was 0.55 for genetic gravity against `divMigrate`'s 0.45 under *Symmetric* migration. Under *Redistributed* migration, genetic gravity had a higher correlation than `divMigrate` on the *plateau* ($\rho =$ 0.52 against 0.39). Similar results were found under *Obstructed* migration ($\rho =$ 0.53 against 0.41).  

Scored on direction alone, $Nm$ did better: on the *plateau* it was larger in the imposed direction on 67% and 62% of the stepping-stone links, against 62% and 59% for $w_{j\mid i}$, and both sat at 50% under *Symmetric* migration. $Nm$ became *unavailable* as a parameter (due to fixation at some loci) before the Population Graph lost information: under *Redistributed* migration it was defined in 98% of censuses in the *ascent* phase core, 59% in the *plateau* phase core and 5% after the horizon, first becoming undefined a median of 175 generations before each lineage's horizon. The agreement of $w_{j\mid i}$ with the truth also fell after the horizon (median 0.31), so neither estimator should be relied on there (Supplemental, S9.2).

## Case Study: Sonoran Desert Bark Beetle

The Population Graph had 34 edges among the 27 sites and four components that recapitulate the regional populations of Garrick et al. (2013; Figure 6): fourteen central sites (24.7–27.4°N), nine northern sites (27.5–29.3°N), and two southern dyads, which carry no directional information and ware not considered further. The central and northern components meet at the hypothesized refugium, so the graph's own partition places the divide where Garrick et al. (2013) located the refugium, consistent with rapid expansion from it in both directions.

*Southward expansion (central component).* Isolation by graph distance was absent under the symmetric model ($R^2_{cGD}=0.007$, Mantel $p=0.23$, 91 pairs) but present under the directional one ($R^2_{dcGD}=0.20$; $\Delta R^2=0.20$). Against a calibrated null, the improvement was significant (one-sided $p=0.008$); without calibration, $\Delta R^2>0$ arose in 38% of symmetric null data sets. Conditional genetic distance accumulated moving north, toward the refuge (reverse slope $7.4\times10^{-3}$ per km), but not moving south, away from it (forward slope $-4.6\times10^{-3}$ per km), the exact pattern expected of a southward expansion. The strongest sources were the sites nearest the refuge (161, SFr and 93; 27.0–27.4°N) and the strongest sinks lay farther south (166, 51 and 77; 24.9–25.9°N), but the gradient test did not reject ($r(S,d)=-0.55$, $p=0.44$; design-matched null $p=0.14$). The reason is the shape of the component: its graph is nearly a star centred on the sites at the refuge, with the sinks as single-edge leaves, so the scores largely restate which sites are hubs (degree share 0.85). Symmetric exchange rarely produced such a star for this design, even when the null reproduced the observed decline in diversity away from the refuge. These conclusions held across bandwidths, locus subsets, and alternative null models (Supplementary Materials, Sections S10.6–S10.9). The data suggest that the central component retains the directional signature of a southward expansion from the refuge, detected by the calibrated directional isolation comparison and consistent with the source–sink scores.

*Northward expansion (northern component).* Isolation by graph distance was strong under the symmetric model ($R^2_{cGD}=0.37$, Mantel $p=0.002$, 36 pairs), yet separate forward and reverse slopes did not improve on it ($\Delta R^2=-0.11$; slope ratio 0.89; calibrated $p=0.96$). The source–sink scores also showed no gradient in this direction ($r(S,d)=0.10$, $p=0.80$), and the degree share (0.36) was typical of the design. With nine sites, however, simualted null distributions for both tests showed little statistical power (Supplementary Materials, Sections S10.2, S10.8). The data suggest that gene flow in the northern component has isolation by distance, consistent with ongoing processes, though no lingering patterns of asymmetric covariance due to post-Pleistocen range expansion.

# Discussion

Genetic gravity converts the conditional topology of a Population Graph into a signed field of directional influence, using only the edge weights the graph already contains and without a demographic model or an assumption of equilibrium. Each undirected edge pools two neighborhood relationships—how typical the edge is from the perspective of each of the two populations it joins—the difference between them, the gravity asymmetry $\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$, is summarized at the population level by the source–sink score $S$. Two tests ask whether that ordering has a direction: a gradient test along a population-level covariate, and a directional isolation comparison that carries the same information into isolation-by-distance and resistance analyses through dcGD. What the framework measures is the relative influence of populations on standing conditional genetic structure. It is not a migration rate *per se*, and a genetic sink is not a demographic sink in the sense of Pulliam (1988); it is best seen as a structural summary that narrows the hypotheses to be examined with model-based estimators, not as a replacement for them.

Among the directional methods reviewed, genetic gravity sits at the low end of assumption load: it needs no demographic model or equilibrium, requires spatial information only when a hypothesis about the axis of flow is tested, works from a single snapshot, and requires only a sparse least-squares solve. It also works with small marker panels (eight loci in the case study, twenty in the simulations), far too few for site-frequency-spectrum methods (e.g., Gutenkunst et al., 2009), so a directional signal can be read from data sets already in hand. The price is that it describes covariate structure rather than estimating microevoluationary rates. However, it adds the ability to separate processes that symmetric summaries conflate. Pairwise differentiation, or weak isolation by distance, can arise from stable symmetric exchange or from directional gene flow, and in both simulations and case study, the symmetric description understated structure that the directional description recovered. The comparison with `divMigrate` shows what that trade means in practice. $Nm$ oriented individual adjacent links in the imposed direction somewhat more often than $w_{j\mid i}$, whereas $w_{j\mid i}$ more cleanly separated the actual corridors, and $Nm$ could no longer be estimated well before the Population Graph lost its structure. The underlying difference is network conditioning. A pairwise estimator evaluates each pair of populations in isolation, so gene flow from $A$ to $C$ routed through $B$ registers as direct exchange between $A$ and $C$ in $Nm$ whereas the Population Graph’s conditional independence reveals $A \leftrightarrow B \leftrightarrow  C$  connectivity without $A \leftrightarrow C$ direct connections.  The clearest result is a separation by scale of inference: the genetic gravity framework is robust at the level of the population and fragile at the level of the individual edge.  Population Graphs, and by extension genetic gravity introduced here, emphasize the full topology over the constructed components and attempt to provide a graph-theoretic construct for subseqent downstream analyses (Dyer & Nason 2004, Baker and Dyer, 2011, DiLeo et al. 2014, Dyer 2015, etc). 

Because graph permuation approaches inflated false positive rates, significance testing for asymmetry was developed as a whole graph approach rather than an edge-by-edge evaluation.  The Source-Sink and Isolation By Graph Distance tests provide complementary inferences (Table 4), both of which are calibrated by the underlying graph topology, differentiating localized and global patterns of asymmetry. The steepness of the source–sink gradient *is not*, however, the strength of the asymmetry as both *Obstructed* and *Redistributed* cases produced nearly the same *plateau* gradient despite a two-fold difference in net migration rate bias.

At very low migration rates (< 0.5 migrants/deme/generation), when genetic drift dominates, the false-positive rate rises dramatically 12–15%.  Directional fit in such a system is weak evidence on its own and is best corroborated by the source–sink test.  Individual edges have no calibrated test (Supplemental, S5.4), so the direction of any one edge is less informative than the patterns of all edges across the topology.  Moreover, a low source-sink score is not by itself evidence of immigration: an isolated population can also read as a sink, so $S$ is best interpreted alongside within-population diversity and node degree.  Some limits are shared by every method that seeks to estimate gene flow from standing variation. When diversity is very low, differentiation reflects the product $N_em$ ($F_{ST}\approx1/(4N_em+1)$) rather than the direction of exchange, and once among-population variance is exhausted neither the presence nor the direction of migration can be statistically recovered through structure-based approaches. Pairwise statistics reach that limit one pair of populations at a time; a Population Graph, estimated jointly, reaches it chornologically later but still reaches it.

The source–sink scores show the state of a system as well as its direction. Under directional flow a single run extends from sources upstream to sinks downstream, and as a *Redistributed* lineage loses information the band of sources moves downstream, from deme 3 to deme 11 over the last 400 generations, a change that no single statistic captures (Figure 3). The steepness of the source–sink gradient *is not*, however, the strength of the asymmetry. *Obstructed* and *Redistributed* lineages produced nearly the same *plateau* gradient despite a two-fold difference in net directional bias, which set how soon a lineage reached its *plateau* rather than how strong the signal was once there. Moreover, the source–sink test depends on a null that preserves the spatial smoothness $S$ inherits from the graph topology: spectral randomization on each census's own Population Graph held its nominal rate from 2.5 down to 0.25 migrants per deme per generation in each direction, whereas nulls that ignored that structure (including one built on the landscape adjacency that generated the data), did not (Supplemental, S5.2). The Population Graph may therefore be a better basis for spatial null hypotehses in research focusing on landscape-genetic style tests. The directional isolation comparison was the more powerful of the two and speaks directly to the isolation and resistance kinds of analyses that Population Graphs feed. The two tests detected partly different censuses, but whenever both rejected they agreed on the direction of flow. ==Table 4 summarizes how to read each combination of outcomes.==

While we can estimate the bandwidth, $\gamma$, across its range, there are two caveats that should be made.  First, iterating across values all values of $\gamma$ should probably be done using a *test set* of loci for estimation, which requires a larger marker set. Second, in the simulation examples, tuning it did not increase power mostly because under directional flow from upstream populations are the true sources.  Whereas in more sparse topologies such as the *Araptus* example, there may be benefit in tune (Supplemental, S10.7) and one should carefuly examine the contribution of node degree when judging the magnitude of $\Delta R^2$ against its simulated rejection region.

These results have practical consequences for study design. The usual advice for population and landscape genetic analysis is to minimize the expected variance in strcuture parameters, roughly $1/\Phi$ per population (Falconer, 1981; Smouse et al. 2001), which here would be four or five individuals.  For a Population Graph, this bumps up against the lower limit sampling for stable estimates of within population genetic variance required for topology estimates.  With sufficient within-population representation, more populations increase the power of the source–sink test, more loci increase the power of both tests, and sites should selected cover the full length of the hypothesized direction of flow or other landscape feature hypothesized to impact connectivity. With fewer than about ten populations, neither test could reliably establish direction (Supplemental, S11). While these numbers come from a one-dimensional simulation with two-allele (SNP-like) loci, a key recommendation is to calibrate all tests by simulating symmetric migration on the observed graph topology.

One challenge with most real data sets is that they are a single snapshot. The *Araptus attenuatus* samples sit at an unknown point in a sequence of states that began with expansion from the refugium after the Last Glacial Maximum and continues to the present. In the simulations, asymmetry was hardest to detect during *ascent* (10–15% of censuses for the source–sink test and about a quarter for the directional comparison), easiest on the *plateau*, and harder again after entering a *decay* phase. At any point in that sequence, a non-significant result is therefore *not evidence* of symmetric migration. The northern *Araptus* component makes the point: Garrick et al. (2013) inferred a northward expansion there from diversity gradients, but with nine sites a graph without a directional signal cannot distinguish symmetric exchange from an expansion too weak to detect.

The framework also points beyond the systems tested here. Repeated sampling of the same populations would turn a single set of source–sink scores into a time series and locate a system within the sequence of states described here. Ecological or directional resistance, such as river networks or prevailing winds, can replace spatial separation in both tests, and each population's local scale could be conditioned on at-site covariates while the multiplier stays fixed (see DiLeo et al., 2014, for this approach applied to pollen-mediated gene flow).

Most inferences about directional gene flow come from systems in which direction is expected along a single axis—expansion from a refugium, downstream drift along a river, dispersal on a prevailing wind—and the simulations and case study here are of that kind; a one-dimensional expansion along the Baja California peninsula after the Pleistocene is not, in itself, surprising. Many landscapes are less simple. Dispersal may run through a two-dimensional network in which a subset of connections carries more movement, and more asymmetric movement, than the rest, along routes set by ecological features or human activity that the researcher may not know in advance. In the western black widow, for example, gene flow among cities forms a network of urban hubs whose connections span far greater distances than those between each city and its surrounding habitat (Miles et al., 2018): part of the dispersal network is highway and part of it back road. A test along a single hypothesized axis is poorly matched to such systems. The source–sink scores, by contrast, are computed from the Population Graph alone, without an axis or covariate, so the source–sink surface can suggest where such channels of directional gene flow lie—canalized paths, in something like Waddington's (1957) sense of a chreod—rather than requiring a hypothesis about them at the outset.

| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Source–sink: reject** | Directional gene flow, supported by both tests.
