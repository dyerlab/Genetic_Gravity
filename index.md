# Abstract

Inferring the directionality of gene flow from genetic data is a long-standing goal in evolutionary and landscape genetics, yet the measures most commonly employed, $F_{ST}$ pairwise genetic distances and model-based migration rates—are either explicitly symmetric or depend on equilibrium assumptions routinely violated in the colonizing, fragmenting, and environmentally filtered systems where directional flow matters most. Population Graphs (Dyer & Nason, 2004) recover the multivariate, conditional structure of genetic covariance as an undirected topology but do not, in canonical form, direct their edges. Here I introduce *genetic gravity*, a node-centric framework that converts a Population Graph’s undirected skeleton into a signed field of directional influence: for every retained edge, a pair of neighbourhood weights $w_{j\mid i}$ and $w_{i\mid j}$ is derived from a Gaussian kernel over conditional genetic distances with a node-specific local bandwidth $b_i$, and their difference, the gravity asymmetry $\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$, partitions the edge into directed components with no new connectivity and no free tuning parameter. Using stochastic individual-based simulations under isotropic and anisotropic migration, I characterize the index from single population-genetic snapshots: a model-comparison test for directional gene flow, usable on a single Population Graph without replicates, that asks whether a directional description of the graph's isolation-by-distance pattern fits better than a symmetric one; its specificity (it remains near zero under symmetric migration however much neutral differentiation accumulates); the sensitivity of its sign and rank to the bandwidth choice; and its behaviour relative to the differentiation-based estimator `divMigrate`, which it complements while avoiding the latter’s drift-driven false positives and fixation artifacts. Genetic gravity offers a topology-derived, equilibrium-free measure of directional connectivity; its longitudinal dynamics are treated in a companion paper. All analyses are implemented in the gstudio R package.

# Introduction

Inferring the pattern and direction of genetic connectivity from molecular data is a central problem in evolutionary, population, and landscape genetics (Slatkin, 1985; Dyer, 2015).  The distribution of population genetic covariance we see today is the result of overlapping evolutionary processes—stochastic, neutral, and adaptive.  A common approach to elucidating the relative importance of these processes is to partition genetic variance into within- and among-stratum components using Wright’s $F_{ST}$ (or one of its many derivatives).  Applying an underlying evolutionary model then allows us to compute the expected values of evolutionarily relevant parameters, such as migration rate (*m*; Slatkin 1981, 1993), genetic effective population size ($N_e$; XXX), inbreeding ($F_{IS}$; XXXX), and others.  This process raises several specific challenges to using the current distribution of genetic variation to infer historical processes.  First, the relationship between a standardized parameter estimated and the stratigraphy of evolutionary and demographic histories that produced the set of sampled alleles and genotypes is non-reversible—the same ratio of within- and among-stratum variances can arise from completely non-overlapping demographic and evolutionary histories.   Second, treating connectivity as a scalar, undirected quantity is analytically convenient, but it homogenizes the varied histories of interactions that created these systems by taking an ‘average across generations under equilibrium conditions’ rather than directly embracing the potential for spatial and temporal heterogeneity within populations.  Estimating the effective number of migrants per generation, *Nem*, is critical for comparative inferences, but may be too coarse when the analytical questions focus on more intricate demographic processes or when attempting to include more granular landscape, demographic, or ecological features that may affect standing genetic structure.  Questions focusing on understanding how genetic structure is affected by colonization, range expansion, source–sink dynamics, wind- or water-mediated dispersal, and environmentally filtered gene flow benefit from approaches that are more fine-grained than a single summarizing parameter, as these inter-strata interactions are heterogeneous and fundamentally *directional* in nature (Baker & Dyer, 2011; Wogan et al., 2024).

The field is not, of course, without tools for addressing questions such as these, and an increasingly sophisticated set of approaches is available for estimating heterogeneity in connectivity and asymmetric gene flow from genetic data. Assignment-based tools such as `BayesAss` infer recent, directional migration rates from multilocus genotypes under linkage and Hardy–Weinberg assumptions (Wilson & Rannala, 2003), while coalescent and isolation-with-migration frameworks recover asymmetric long-term migration matrices, either by direct likelihood over genealogies (Beerli & Felsenstein, 2001) or by fitting the joint site-frequency spectrum of a few populations (e.g., Gutenkunst et al., 2009). Where the question is specifically the direction of a range expansion, the directionality index $\psi$ reads the origin and axis of spread from pairwise allele-frequency asymmetries (Peter & Slatkin, 2013). At the landscape scale, effective-migration-surface methods map spatial heterogeneity in gene flow (Marcus et al., 2021; Petkova et al., 2016), and recent extensions of that family relax their symmetry assumption to render the surface directional. Each of these is powerful within its domain, but each also exacts a characteristic cost: a prespecified demographic or spatial model, an assumption of migration–drift equilibrium, genome-scale marker panels, a small number of demes, explicit spatial coordinates, or substantial computational resources. This manuscript provides a lightweight, assumption-free summary of *directional structure* that can be read directly from the multivariate covariance dependency topology and scales to many populations from a single snapshot. The framework extended herein is intended to fill this niche by using conditional genetic covariance structures, as represented within Population Graphs (Dyer & Nason, 2004; Dyer, 2007, 2015), to identify and quantify asymmetry in population migration.

Population Graphs offer a partial escape from the symmetry assumption using multilocus conditional genetic covariance within a graph-theoretic model. Rather than estimating a single pairwise statistic that describes the average among-stratum variance structure, the Population Graph framework translates the among-stratum variance into a weighted network of connectivity using conditional genetic covariance.  Population Graphs are distinct from pairwise structure or genetic distance analyses in one important way.  Pairwise approaches focus on estimates from each combinatorial pair of strata and, for example, reconstruct a relationship with pairwise distance (such as Slatkin’s $\hat{M}$ 19XX) to estimate isolation.  Conversely, estimates of stratum-to-stratum covariance in a Population Graph are based on the multilocus covariance between the two populations in question, statistically conditional on their covariance structures with the remaining populations in the analysis.  This resulting covariance topology captures dependencies that pairwise measures obscure. Moreover, the networks shape is diagnostic of the evolutionary processes that produced it—range expansions, vicariance, and equilibrium isolation-by-distance each leave characteristic signatures in node degree, edge length, and network diameter (Dyer, 2007, 2015). Population Graphs have been used to address questions of connectivity and structure in a broad range of scenarios including landscape genetics (Daniel et al., 2024; Dyer, 2015), conservation planning (Vilaça et al., 2024), biogeography (Wogan et al., 2024), life-history effects (Reed et al., 2023), invasive species management (Baker & Dyer, 2011), and pollen-mediated gene flow (Dileo et al., 2014; Dyer et al., 2004; Smouse et al., 2001).

The edges in a Population Graph are weighted estimates of conditional inter-population covariance: the conditional covariance $e_{ij}$ is a scalar, and the overall genetic covariance topology produced by the approach is, by definition, undirected.  Both source and sink populations may share a strong conditional dependency, but the *direction* of that dependency—who is overwriting whose allele frequencies—is a biologically distinct question that cannot be read directly from the undirected edge any more easily than it can be addressed with pairwise structure statistics. The same edge weight can be produced by a stable, symmetric exchange and by a unidirectional colonization pulse, yet the evolutionary implications are opposite, and in most cases, the actual inference of highest practical interest to the researcher.

Here, I introduce the concept of *genetic gravity*, an extension of the topology of the classic Population Graph that creates a node-centric framework for extracting directional information from an otherwise undirected Population Graph.  The goal here is to determine whether the conditional genetic structure encoded in the Population Graph topology itself can be used to partition symmetric covariance into asymmetric components, without invoking any new population-genetic model or incorporating additional outside information.  To this end,I begin by formalizing the notion of genetic-gravity deriving a pair of directional weights ($w_{j\mid i}$ and $w_{i\mid j}$) for each edge ($e_{ij}$) to define an asymmetry index, $\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$, associated with each edge.  For this, I introduce a node-local Gaussian kernel approach whose bandwidth is read directly from the Population Graph.  Second, I characterize the parameters' behavior—highlighting boundary effects that arise at low-degree nodes—under both stochastic models of symmetric and asymmetric connectivity. I validate the framework with stochastic individual-based simulations under three migration regimes—isotropic, redistributed anisotropic, and obstructed anisotropic—tracking both the canonical undirected Population Graph metrics and their directed counterparts derived from genetic gravity, as well as a mean asymmetry index $\bar{\Delta}$. Next, I characterize the temporal dynamics and detectability of the directional signal against a within-replicate empirical null, using a standardized mixed-effects framework that explicitly models the strong autocorrelation of densely sampled graph trajectories.  Finally, I benchmark the asymmetry index against an existing approach suitable for the kinds of data found in the simulations using `divMigrate`, a widely used differentiation-based estimator of directional relative migration (Sundqvist et al., 2016).  The comparison is to evaluate the similarity in response of the two approaches, the sensitivity to changes in the underlying population covariance matrix driven by asymmetry in connectivity, and the relative accuracy at which each can approximate the true migration matrix used in the simulations.  All simulation routines and analysis functions have been implemented in the `gstudio` R package maintained by the author and are freely available. Although the simulations and worked examples here use simple spatial position along a chain, neither test is tied to Euclidean geography: substituting a resistance-based or other ecological distance for the spatial separation used throughout is a direct extension of the framework to the kinds of landscape-genetic questions Population Graphs are already well suited to (Dyer, 2015).
# Methods
The directional framework proposed here assumes that a Population Graph has already been constructed from multilocus genetic data; the relevant inputs are the symmetric edge weights $e_{ij}$ and the adjacency structure of the graph $G=\{V,E\}$ retained by the construction of this Markov independence graph. Notation follows the *Q*-mode formulation of Dyer et al. (2004) and the original Population Graph derivation in Dyer & Nason (2004); a compact summary sufficient for the derivation below is collected in the Supplementary Materials (Tab_Notation).
## The Model of Genetic Gravity
A Population Graph is a Markov independence graph and possesses several statistical features, two of which are relevant to the development of asymmetry models. First, a core property of all independence graphs concerns *graph separation*. Any pair of populations *directly* connected in a graph topology (e.g., $A\leftrightarrow B$ denoted by the edge $e_{AB}$) are, by definition, not genetically independent. Evolutionary processes that lead to conditional dependence include ongoing genetic connectivity or recent divergence from a common ancestral population.  Cessation of connectivity through time allows genetic drift and other processes to lead populations towards genetic independence. If populations are connected only *through* one or more other populations, in a stepwise fashion (e.g., $A\leftrightarrow B\leftrightarrow C$), populations not directly connected (such as $A$ and $C$) are, in population graphs, conditionally independent such that $P(A,C\mid B)=P(A\mid B)P(C\mid B)$.

Second, nodes in a Population Graph have pairwise *Markov independence*, meaning that any two non-adjacent populations are genetically independent, conditional to *all other populations* in the topology. If a sampling population is added or removed from the data set, the entire topology must be re-evaluated because the presence (and absence) of each edge depends on *all the data simultaneously*, not just the populations attached to that edge. This is one feature of Population Graphs that distinguishes them from more traditional pairwise structure estimators commonly used in evolutionary, landscape, and population genetic analyses. Markov independence is also reflexive: each target population is evolutionarily independent of all other populations in the topology, *given its* direct neighbors. Biologically, this guarantees that an edge weight reflects a genuine, direct evolutionary relationship between the two populations it connects, rather than an artifact of which other populations happened to be included in the sample—a property that matters when attributing a directional signal to a specific corridor of gene flow rather than incidental sampling design artifcacts. 
## Directional Weights
Each symmetric edge in a Population Graph is the pooled product of two conditional relationships: the contribution of population $j$ to the genetic structure of $i$, and the symmetric and opposite contribution of $i$ to $j$.  The edge weight, $e_{ij}$,  has been used as a measure of conditional genetic distance (cGD) to infer isolation by graph distance (Dyer 2015; Miles et al. 2018).

Focusing on each target node and its collection of neighboring edges ($N(i)$), I define a *neighbourhood weight*, $w_{j\mid i}$, as $j$'s weight in $i$'s neighbourhood: the conditional probability that $j$ is the most influential neighbor of $i$ given the other nodes in $N(i)$, using a Gaussian Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (Gaussian SNE; Hinton & Roweis, 2002) approach where:

$$
w_{j\mid i}= \frac{exp\left({- e_{ij}^{2}/2b_i^{2}}\right)}{\sum\limits_{k\in N(i)}^{}exp\left({- e_{ik}^{2}/2b_i^{2}}\right)}
$$

where $e_{ij}$ is the symmetric conditional-covariance edge weight from the Population Graph and $b_{i}$ is a node-specific *local bandwidth* parameter. The kernel is strictly local: only retained neighboring nodes, $j\in N(i)$ contribute to the denominator, so nodes that are conditionally independent of $i$ in $G$ (e.g., those beyond the first degree neighbors) contribute nothing due to the Markov properties of the Population Graph.  The bandwidth $b_{i}$ controls the rate at which the Gaussian kernel in Equation 1 decays with distance: a large $b_{i}$ spreads influence broadly across the neighborhood, while a small $b_{i}$ concentrates it on the nearest neighbor. Intuitively, a node whose retained neighbors are genetically more divergent, whether because it occupies a bridging position between differentiated clusters in the connectivity network or because it lies at the edge of a stepping-stone chain, receives a broad kernel that distributes its neighbourhood weight proportionally over that extended neighborhood, whereas a node embedded in a tight cluster of genetically similar populations receives a narrow kernel that concentrates its signal on its closest retained neighbors.  I set the local bandwidth to the mean edge weight over node $i$’s retained neighborhood:

$$
b_{i} = \frac{s_{i}}{k_{i}} = \frac{1}{k_{i}}\sum\limits_{j\in N(i)}^{}e_{ij}
$$

where $s_{i}=\sum\limits_{j\in N(i)}^{}e_{ij}$ is the *strength* (weighted degree) of node $i$.  Moreover, this parameter is scale-equivariant: for any constant $c>0$, rescaling every conditional distance $e_{ij}\mapsto c e_{ij}$ sends $b_{i}\mapsto c b_{i}$, leaves each exponent $e_{ij}^{2}/2b_i^{2}$ fixed, and hence

$$
w_{j\mid i}(c e)=w_{j\mid i}(e),  \; \; \Delta_{i\rightarrow j}(c e)=\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}(e) \;\;  (c>0).
$$
In practice, this means $\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$ captures relative directional imbalance rather than absolute genetic differentiation: two Population Graphs built from markers with very different overall levels of divergence—or the same graph with every distance rescaled by an arbitrary constant—will agree on which edges are directional and by how much in relative terms, so the index is comparable across datasets and marker panels rather than being an artifact of how much raw differentiation happens to be present.
## Genetic Gravity: The Asymmetry Index
For every edge $e_{ij}\in E$, the two neighbourhood weights that compare it are computed independently from the two nodes' local bandwidths:
$$
w_{j\mid i}=\frac{exp(-e_{ij}^{2}/2b_i^{2})}{\sum\limits_{k\in N(i)}^{}exp(-e_{ik}^{2}/2b_i^{2})}, \; \; \; \;  w_{i\mid j}=\frac{exp(-e_{ij}^{2}/2b_j^{2})}{\sum\limits_{k\in N(j)}^{}exp(-e_{jk}^{2}/2b_j^{2})}
$$
Because $b_{i}\ne b_{j}$ in general, the weights differ. The signed difference
$$
\Delta_{i\rightarrow j} = w_{i\mid j}-w_{j\mid i}
$$
is the *gravity asymmetry* along the edge—$i$'s pull on $j$ minus $j$'s pull on $i$. Positive $\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$ means $i$ holds more weight in $j$'s neighbourhood than $j$ holds in $i$'s: $j$'s kernel points more strongly back at $i$ than the reverse, a signature consistent with $i$ acting as a genetic source and $j$ as a sink. A value near zero indicates a symmetric balanced exchange; a large estimate of $|\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}|$ indicates a strongly directional edge (the sign of which determines direction). Throughout, higher values mean more of a source: this holds for $\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$ and for every node-level quantity built from it below (*Genetic gravity at the population level*), and no asymmetry or population-level score in the paper carries the opposite sign. The one directional quantity that reads the other way is the partitioned distance pGD (below): because it is a distance, it is shorter in the direction of gene flow. "Source" and "sink" here are genetic, not demographic: a genetic source is a population whose allele frequencies its neighbours have been pulled toward, read from standing conditional genetic structure, not a count of migrants; this is distinct from the demographic sense of Pulliam (1988), where a sink is a population with $\lambda<1$ maintained by immigration. Low gravity is not by itself proof of immigration—an isolated, strongly drifted population can read as a sink for the same reason a true recipient does, because "sink" means weak genetic gravity, which directional immigration produces but isolation can mimic; interpreting a low score is best done alongside within-population diversity.

It is worth being explicit about how a *directed* quantity can arise from an object that is symmetric by construction. The conditional-covariance structure underlying a Population Graph, and the edge weights $e_{ij}$ read from it, are symmetric (e.g., $e_{ij}=e_{ji}$); no manipulation of a single edge weight can yield a direction. The asymmetry index does not come from the edge itself, it comes from the two *neighborhoods the edge is compared against*. The local bandwidth $b_{i}$ is node $i$'s native genetic-distance scale, the mean conditional distance over its retained neighborhood, and the neighbourhood weight $w_{j\mid i}$ measures how near edge $(i,j)$ is *relative to that scale*. The same edge weight $e_{ij}$ therefore reads as relatively near from $i$'s side when $b_i$ is large (when $i$'s other neighbours are, on average, farther away) and relatively far from $j$'s side when $b_j$ is small, and the difference between these two relative readings, $\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$, determines whether the edge is more typical of $i$'s local scale or of $j$'s. The direction this comparison actually reads, however, is not intuitive from bandwidth alone. A naive reading would have a source compress its sinks' neighbourhoods and so carry the larger bandwidth itself; the opposite holds. Under imposed gene flow the source more often carries the *smaller* own bandwidth (57–59% of edges at peak asymmetry), because its own neighbours increasingly resemble it, compressing its own neighbourhood scale. Separately, and even under symmetric migration, a larger own bandwidth leans toward a more source-like (positive) own $\Delta$, because a flatter kernel spreads a node's weight away from its nearest neighbour, lowering the weight it places on that neighbour relative to the weight the neighbour places back on it. The two effects pull against each other—sources tend to carry the smaller bandwidth, yet a smaller bandwidth on its own leans sink-like—so the directional signal in $\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$ comes from which neighbours are close, not from the bandwidth contrast. Bandwidth contrast is therefore not a proxy for which population is the source; the sign should be read from the index, not assumed from the bandwidths in isolation. For more on the separability of directional signal from node degree, see Supplementary Materials, *Bandwidth is Not Degree*.

The same two neighbourhood weights that define $\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$ also let the edge's conditional genetic distance itself be split directionally. Partitioning $e_{ij}$ in proportion to $w_{j\mid i}$ and $w_{i\mid j}$ yields a *partitioned conditional genetic distance* (pGD): $p_{ij} = e_{ij}\,w_{j\mid i}/(w_{j\mid i}+w_{i\mid j})$, and correspondingly $p_{ji} = e_{ij}\,w_{i\mid j}/(w_{j\mid i}+w_{i\mid j})$, so that $p_{ij}+p_{ji}=e_{ij}$, with $p_{ij}=p_{ji}=e_{ij}/2$ under symmetric connectivity. Because pGD is a distance, its direction reads opposite to the source–sink convention: when $i$ is a source for $j$ ($\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}>0$, so $w_{i\mid j}>w_{j\mid i}$), $p_{ij}<p_{ji}$—conditional distance is shorter in the direction of gene flow. Unlike $e_{ij}$ itself, which is symmetric by construction, pGD assigns each direction of an edge its own share of the conditional distance, turning the Population Graph's otherwise-undirected edge set into a directed, asymmetrically-weighted graph over the same topology—used directly in the comparison with `divMigrate` below.

Classic population genetic theory provides some expectations for the asymmetry statistic in discrete populations under migration–drift processes. Comparing systems with isotropic and anisotropic gene flow, we expect diverging responses in the trajectory of resident allele frequencies. In isotropic systems, the entire metapopulation is connected and will, eventually, tend to $\bar{p}$, minimizing structure and erasing any evidence of isolation by distance. The introduction of asymmetry will be impacted by two factors: 1) the magnitude of $m$, and 2) the relative ratio of asymmetry in $m_{i\rightarrow i+1}$ vs. $m_{i\leftarrow i+1}$. These act on different aspects of the system's response: the relative difference between the two rates sets the direction and strength of the net force driving the metapopulation away from a differentiated topology, while the absolute magnitude of both rates together sets how quickly the system moves along that trajectory, independent of direction. Two migration regimes with the same directional imbalance but different total flux are therefore expected to approach the same eventual state at different rates, and this should be visible both in how quickly the asymmetry index itself develops and in how quickly the graph's topology responds.

## Genetic gravity at the population level

The edge-level asymmetry index compares two populations at a time, but the comparisons it makes are not independent: an edge's neighbourhood weight is read against the same node-specific bandwidth used by every other edge touching that node, so edges sharing a population are expected to move together rather than behave as separate pieces of evidence. This motivates a population-level quantity, *gravity*, that summarizes how strongly a population's neighbours resemble it—how much weight it holds, in aggregate, in their neighbourhoods:
$$
g_{i} = \sum\limits_{j\in N(i)}^{} w_{i\mid j}
$$
Because each population's own neighbourhood weights ($w_{j\mid i}$ over $j\in N(i)$) sum to 1 by construction, gravity is conserved across the graph: $\sum_{i} g_{i}=K$ for any Population Graph without isolated populations. The *source–sink balance*, $g_{i}-1$, therefore sums to zero over the graph whatever the migration pattern—every population that reads as a source is balanced by populations that read as sinks. A population sits at $g_{i}=1$ when it holds exactly as much weight in its neighbours' neighbourhoods as it gives them in its own; $g_{i}-1$ is positive for a source and negative for a sink. Individual populations can depart from 1 through degree alone, even under symmetric migration (the topological floor, below).

Gravity aggregates a population's edges, but it does not by itself place populations on a common directional scale the way a single ordering would. The *source–sink score*, $S_{i}$, is the population-level value whose pairwise differences best reproduce the observed gravity asymmetries along every retained edge simultaneously, $S_{i}-S_{j}\approx\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$, fit by least squares over the graph (Supplementary Materials). A high $S$ marks a source; this is the quantity mapped onto geography and tested against a hypothesized covariate (*Significance Testing for Asymmetry*, below).

The population, not the edge, is the natural unit of inference for three reasons. First, a single source-to-sink ordering of the populations reproduces the large majority (81–85%) of the variation in edge-level asymmetries, confirming that edges sharing a population are not independent evidence but repeated readings of the same underlying node-level quantity. Second, $S$ requires no edge orientation to be chosen in advance—it is fit from the undirected topology and the signed $\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$ values together. Third, $S$ compares populations only within a connected component of the graph, which matches how a Population Graph can itself fragment under strong asymmetry or collapse (*Individual-based simulations*, above).

Gravity has a topological floor. Under symmetric migration $S$ tracks node degree closely (correlation magnitude $\approx 0.8$): hubs score as sources and leaves as sinks, independent of any directional gene flow. Asymmetric migration decouples the two (correlation magnitude falls to about 0.49 under Redistributed and 0.61 under Obstructed connectivity), so the degree-driven floor and the migration-driven signal are separable empirically, though not perfectly, the same way the edge-level topological term $\Delta^{0}_{i\rightarrow j}$ separates from the directional remainder (Supplementary Materials, *Bandwidth is Not Degree*).

## Bandwidth sensitivity

Because the local bandwidth $b_{i}=s_{i}/k_{i}$ is itself a modeling choice, its sensitivity was assessed directly: $\Delta$ was recomputed on the forward-phase snapshots under three departures from this canonical local bandwidth—a fixed *global* bandwidth (a single scale, the mean retained edge weight, applied to every node), the local bandwidth *scaled* by $\frac{1}{2}$ and by $2$ (a sharper and a flatter kernel), and a *perplexity*-based per-node bandwidth (a tSNE-style $\sigma$ search)—and compared, edge by edge and at the graph-mean level, to the canonical estimator (Results; Supplementary Materials).

## Individual-based simulations
The sensitivity and temporal behavior of the asymmetry index were evaluated using *individual-based stochastic simulation* following Dyer (2007). Every replicate run tracks a metapopulation of $K=25$ discrete demes, each of which has $N=100$ diploid individuals with $L=20$ independently segregating, codominant, biallelic loci.  Each replicate run of the simulations started with random allele frequencies.  Generations were simulated as non-overlapping and all mating is random within the population.  Mutation was excluded to isolate drift–migration dynamics. Migrants were selected at random each generation according to the fixed migration rate. Dyer (2007) showed using similar indiviudal-based forward time simulations that graph-theoretic parametes such as average degree centrality ($\bar{C}_D$) and graph diameter ($\bar{d}_G$) stabilized much quicker than population genetic structure ($\Phi_{ST}$) or patterns of isolation by distance ($\hat{M}$).  While the primary focus of this paper is extending the Population Graph focus, we will be comparing the existence of asymmetry ($\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$) from this framework to an approach that relies on genetic structure. Consequently, each replicate run will use a 2,000-generation burn-in of symmetric gene flow on a one-dimensional symmetric stepping-stone topology ($m_{i\rightarrow i+1}=m_{i\leftarrow i+1}=0.025$, where the population pair is always ordered $(i,i{+}1)$ and the arrow alone indicates forward versus reverse flow).  The previous work suggested this would be sufficient for burn in using both graph-theoretic and population genetic metrics.  Simulations were checked to ensure they were in drift–migration equilibrium.  Fifty replicates were run to provide confidence on resulting patterns.

A second asymmetry scenario was also added, an *obstructed* asymmetry which held forward migration rate fixed but suppressed reverse migration by 60% ($m_{i\rightarrow i+1} = 0.025$; $m_{i\leftarrow i+1} = 0.01$), modeling ambient-rate forward dispersal (as in the *isotropic* case) against a partially obstructed return path connectivity.  Each asymmetric case was designed to deviate from the paired isotoropic simulation in exactly one parameter so their contrasts would elucidated orthogonal drivers on the parameter $\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$: isotropic versus redistributed isolates directional balance at fixed total flux rates and isotropic versus obstructed isolates back-flow suppression at fixed forward rate.  Comparisons between the redistributed and obstructed scenarios also uncovers the incremental effect of forward-rate migration elevation. Because all three scenarios branch from the same burn-in state within each replicate, the isotropic trajectory at each forward generation serves as a within-replicate empirical, fixed evolutionary trajectory null. 

There are several expectations and discrete phases in the evolution of population structure expected to emerge from these scenarios. The first phase (*Burn In*) allows all replicates to start from random initial conditions and, given the underlying migration matrix, gain stability in both within- and among-population covariance, measured using either graph-theoretic or population genetic parameters; because migration is symmetric throughout this phase, its endpoint is the only point in the simulation at which a genuinely symmetric migration–drift equilibrium is guaranteed to hold, and it is therefore the appropriate reference point for characterizing the false-positive rate of any significance test intended for later use in the forward phase, before drift-driven reorganization has had a chance to accumulate. The application of a modified migration matrix enters the populations into a second phase, *Reorganization*, in which population covariance is restructured in response to the new pattern and rate of connectivity; this phase would not be observed in the *Isotropic* scenario but would be evident in both the *Redistributed* and *Obstructed* lineages. Because each replicate enters this phase from the same burn-in-equilibrated starting configuration but then evolves as an independent stochastic realization, replicates are not expected to reorganize in lock-step: each replicate's Population Graph structure moves toward the imposed asymmetry at its own rate, governed by which lineages happen to drift toward fixation first, so the variance across replicates in any snapshot-based statistic is itself expected to change over the forward phase—rising as an increasing fraction of replicates begin losing diversity while others have not, reaching a zenith, and falling again as the remaining replicates catch up. This replicate-specific pacing is why lineages are aligned on their own informative state—remaining genetic diversity, or each replicate's own peak in the asymmetry index—rather than on generation time, whenever the question is the shape of the reorganization signal itself rather than its population-level average timing. The next phase is relative *Stability* under the new migration matrix, analogous to the stability sought at the end of the Burn In phase, though whether it is reached within a finite observation window depends on the strength of the imposed asymmetry. Finally, in a closed migration–drift system without mutation, a sustained directional bias in pairwise migration rate is expected, in the limit as $t\rightarrow\infty$, to homogenize allele frequencies across the chain rather than sustain a differentiated topology—for two exchanging demes with $m_{i\rightarrow i+1}>m_{i\leftarrow i+1}$, classical migration theory gives $p_{i+1}\rightarrow p_{i}$ as $t\rightarrow\infty$, with reverse migration only slowing that convergence—so in one-dimensional stepping-stone models at these levels of asymmetry, genetic homogenization is expected to cascade in the direction of dominant migration, and in the limit all populations converge and both diversity and structure collapse. This last phase, *Collapse*, has two distinct empirical signatures as loci approach fixation. First, the conditional genetic covariance from which a Population Graph is estimated degenerates as allele frequencies homogenize, so retained edges are progressively lost and, past some point, a graph can no longer be estimated at all—a structural failure that is itself diagnostic of how far a lineage has progressed. Second, differentiation-based statistics that normalize by a measure of genetic differentiation between populations are expected to become numerically unstable as that differentiation collapses toward zero at fixed pairs, returning undefined, negative, or spuriously extreme values rather than failing outright (Results, Comparison to `divMigrate`). The two behaviors matter differently for inference: a Population Graph that fails to build signals its own limits directly, whereas a differentiation-normalized statistic can return a value that looks like an ordinary estimate without signaling that it no longer reflects genuine biological signal. The time it takes each replicate to pass through these stages is therefore expected to be lineage-specific, depending on how, and how much, its own evolutionary trajectory has distributed genetic variation within and among populations.

To help identify these stages in each replicate, a snapshot of multilocus genotypes was retained every five generations. The genotypes of each snapshot were converted into a Population Graph (Dyer & Nason 2004).  In addition to directional weights and asymmetry indices, edge density, graph diameter, and mean conditional genetic distance (cGD) were retained for each snapshot for compison to structure and isolation by distance inferences after Dyer (2007).  

## Significance Testing for Asymmetry

In the simulations the direction of gene flow is known because it was imposed; in an empirical study it is not. Estimating a Population Graph and fitting isolation by graph distance (IBGD) to its conditional genetic distances is already routine, and the question of asymmetry arises only when a researcher suspects directional gene flow or wants to know whether it is present. Both tests below require a hypothesized covariate or axis to test against—this is a design choice that matches how Population Graphs are already used for isolation-by-distance and related questions, not a limitation particular to these tests. In these simulations that covariate is simply ordered position along the simulated chain, and the corresponding distance is Euclidean, but neither test is Euclidean-specific: nothing prevents the population-level covariate $x$ or the pairwise distance $|dx_{ij}|$ from being an ecological or resistance-based distance of the kind already routine in landscape genetics, in place of geographic position and Euclidean separation (Dyer, 2015). That substitution is a direct extension of both tests to environmentally mediated connectivity, not a different method.

Permutation and sign-exchangeability nulls built directly on $\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$ or on $S$ were evaluated first and rejected (Supplementary Materials, *Permutation nulls for $\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$*): every null that shuffles or resamples parts of a single already-estimated Population Graph—rewiring edges, permuting individuals onto a fixed topology, flipping edge signs, or permuting $S$ across nodes—was anti-conservative under symmetric migration, from 8–15% up to 100% false positives at nominal $\alpha=0.05$, depending on the null. The underlying reason is structural rather than a defect of any one null: a Population Graph is a Markov random field, so an edge's weight already conditions on the rest of the topology, and the same conditioning makes $\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$ and $S$ inherit smooth, node-level structure (degree, bandwidth, and a drift-generated spatial trend along the graph) that no within-graph exchangeability null reproduces, because exchanging parts of one graph among themselves cannot regenerate the correlation those parts already share by construction. This is not fixable by a better choice of null within that family—the node-level structure is the problem, not incidental noise—so it does not represent evidence against asymmetry, but it does mean that node-level structure being reproducible at all requires a null that preserves it rather than shuffling it away. Two tests do that, evaluated below in the order a researcher would apply them: a spectrally-calibrated test on the source–sink score $S$ that asks whether directional structure exists at all and where, followed by a model-comparison test that asks whether modelling that structure explicitly improves the graph's isolation-by-distance fit.

### Source–sink gradient test

This test asks whether the population-level score $S$ tracks a hypothesized covariate $x$—and, because $S$ is resolved per population, which populations are the sources. It needs no directional distances to be constructed and no axis to be assumed in advance beyond the covariate itself, so it is the natural first check: a rejection identifies both that a gradient exists and, from the sign of $S$ against $x$, which direction it runs, motivating the more specific directional isolation model below along that same axis. The test statistic is Spearman's $r(S,x)$. Because high $S$ marks a source, $r(S,x)<0$ places the sources at low values of $x$ (upstream, when $x$ increases downstream) and $r(S,x)>0$ places them at high values. Its null preserves $S$'s spectral power on the census's own Population Graph rather than exchanging parts of it: Moran spectral randomization (`adespatial::msr`, "singleton" method) on the kernel-weighted adjacency $\exp(-e_{ij}^{2}/2\bar b^{2})$, where $\bar b$ is the mean retained edge weight, with $B=999$ randomizations, tested two-sided at $\alpha=0.05$. For a disconnected graph, randomization is applied within each connected component separately. This null is what makes the test valid where the exchangeability nulls above were not: preserving the graph's own spectral structure, rather than shuffling or permuting it away, reproduces the node-level smoothness that $S$ inherits from drift alone, so that only a genuine departure from it—an actual gradient along the hypothesized covariate—registers as a rejection. The false-positive rate of this test was measured on censuses simulated under symmetric migration: the final 100 generations of burn-in (every 5 generations, 1,000 censuses), the isotropic forward phase, and three further symmetric scenarios at progressively lower migration that raise neutral differentiation without imposing a direction (Supplementary Materials, *Differentiation without Direction*); the forward-phase samples were taken every 50 generations (1,000 censuses per scenario). Power was measured as the rejection rate on the same census grid under the redistributed and obstructed treatments, by 250-generation forward-phase window.

### Directional isolation by graph distance

Where the source–sink gradient test asks whether directional structure exists and identifies which populations carry it, this test asks whether modelling that direction explicitly improves how well the graph's topology explains isolation by distance—the use Population Graphs are already routinely put to. We treat it as a comparison between two isolation models fit to the same Population Graph—one that assumes gene flow is symmetric and one that allows it to be directional. A gradient detected by the source–sink test motivates trying this comparison along the corresponding axis; the procedure below follows the order a researcher would apply it once that motivation is in hand, though it can equally be applied on its own:

1. **Estimate the topology and fit standard IBGD.** The Population Graph is estimated as usual, and all-pairs conditional genetic distance (cGD, the shortest-path distance over edge weights $e_{ij}$) is regressed on pairwise spatial separation, $|dx_{ij}|$, giving $R^2_{cGD}$. If asymmetry is not suspected, the analysis ends here.
2. **Estimate the directional distances.** If asymmetry is suspected, the directed graph with arc costs $p_{ij}$ (pGD; *Genetic Gravity: The Asymmetry Index*) is formed and all-pairs directed shortest-path distances are computed, so that the distance from $i$ to $j$ may differ from the distance from $j$ to $i$.
3. **Fit a directional isolation model.** A directional model requires direction on both sides of the regression: the genetic response (pGD, over ordered pairs $i\neq j$) and the spatial separation. Spatial direction is supplied by a hypothesized axis of flow—downstream, a prevailing wind or current, or the front of a range expansion; here, the one-dimensional stepping-stone chain—so that each ordered pair is either *forward* ($j$ lies downstream of $i$) or *reverse*. The model $\text{pGD}_{ij} \sim |dx_{ij}| + |dx_{ij}| \cdot r_{ij}$, where $r_{ij}=1$ for reverse pairs, gives pGD separate forward and reverse isolation slopes, with fit $R^2_{pGD}$ (adjusted for the additional slope).
4. **Compare the fits.** If $\Delta R^2 = R^2_{pGD} - R^2_{cGD} > 0$, the directional description of the graph explains isolation better than the symmetric one, which is consistent with asymmetric gene flow along the hypothesized axis. If $\Delta R^2 \le 0$, the symmetric description is sufficient; this is an absence of evidence for asymmetry, not evidence of symmetry.
5. **Describe the asymmetry.** Where the directional fit is supported, the ratio of the forward to the reverse slope, $b_{fwd}/b_{rev}$, gives the direction and relative strength of isolation along the axis: a ratio below one means genetic distance accumulates more slowly downstream than upstream.

Because $R^2_{cGD}$ and $R^2_{pGD}$ are fit to different response matrices, the comparison is not a nested-model test; it asks which representation of the same Population Graph—symmetric or directional—geography describes better. Its error rates were therefore characterized empirically. The false-positive rate is the fraction of censuses with $\Delta R^2>0$ under symmetric migration, using both the final 100 generations of burn-in (twenty 5-generation censuses per replicate, 1,000 censuses in total; Individual-based simulations) and the entire isotropic forward phase (200 censuses per replicate, 10,000 in total). Detection was measured as the same fraction under the redistributed and obstructed treatments, summarized by forward-phase window so that the rate can be read against the reorganization, stability, and collapse phases described above.

## Comparison with a differentiation-based method
To evaluate the sensitivity of the asymmetry statistic, $\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$,  the data were also evaluated against an existing tool that ingests the same kinds of genetic data used for these individual-based simululations, `divMigrate` (Sundqvist et al., 2016).  This method estimates directional relative migration between population pairs by estimating genetic differentiation and is among the most widely used network-level descriptors of asymmetric gene flow that is applied to population-level sampling with co-dominant multilocus genotypes. The statistic used throughout this comparison, divMigrate's $N_m$, is not a classical migration-rate estimator despite the name; it is Alcalá et al.'s (2014) formula, which combines harmonic-mean $G_{ST}$ and Jost's $D$, and is the statistic Sundqvist et al. (2016) report behaves most like $G_{ST}$, their recommended choice. 

As noted in the Introduction, the Population Graph's minimal topology and divMigrate's exhaustive pairwise estimation are not directly comparable: the Population Graph retains only a subset of the $K(K-1)/2$ potential pairs as edges, and the more spatial genetic structure present in the system, the fewer edges remain (Dyer 2007). Comparing the two therefore required translating the Population Graph's edge weights into a form defined over all pairs (pGD, above), and the comparison was examined from the following two perspectives.  

**Similarity in the evolution of asymmetry.** The pGD-weighted directed graph (*Genetic Gravity: The Asymmetry Index*) formed the basis for this comparison: all-pairs directed shortest-path distances $P_{ij}$ were computed across it, treating pGD as arc cost, rescaled to $[0,1]$, and converted to a bounded similarity $\sigma_{ij}=1/(1+P_{ij})$, producing a dense $K\times K$ matrix comparable in form to divMigrate's output. We compared $\sigma_{ij}$ to divMigrate's $N_m$-based relative migration matrix [@Sundqvist2016] at matching census points using Spearman's $\rho$ over the off-diagonal pairs, without significance testing; pairs unreachable in the Population Graph or undefined by divMigrate were excluded. This comparison was carried out across all three treatments — isotropic, redistributed, and obstructed — to assess whether the two independently derived measures of directional structure track one another, and how closely, over the course of the simulation. 

**Correlation with the underlying migration matrix.** The parameter $\sigma_{ij}$ (Similarity in the evolution of asymmetry, above) and `divMigrate`'s $N_m$ agree with one another, but agreement between two indirect estimators cannot by itself establish that either one is reading the real underlying process. Because the forward-phase simulations impose a known, fixed migration schedule (Tab_ScenarioParameters), the true directional migration matrix is available independently of any inference drawn from the simulated genotypes: for every scenario, $m_{i\rightarrow j}$ equals the scenario's forward or reverse rate on each stepping-stone link and zero for every non-adjacent pair.  These rates were held constant across the entire forward phase of the simulations. This lets `divMigrate`'s $N_m$ and genetic gravity's own neighbourhood weight, $w_{j\mid i}$, each be tested directly against ground truth rather than each other. Gene flow from $j$ to $i$ pulls $i$'s allele frequencies toward $j$'s and so raises $j$'s weight in $i$'s neighbourhood, $w_{j\mid i}$; the neighbourhood weight is therefore compared against the transposed truth, $m_{j\rightarrow i}$. `divMigrate`'s relative migration matrix is read from row to column—entry $[i,j]$ is relative migration from $i$ to $j$ (Sundqvist et al., 2016)—and is compared against $m_{i\rightarrow j}$ directly. With these orientations a positive gravity asymmetry $\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$ and a positive `divMigrate` asymmetry, $N_m[i,j]-N_m[j,i]$, both mark $i$ as the source. Because the true matrix is zero on all but the 48 stepping-stone pairs, agreement over all ordered pairs mainly measures whether an estimator separates corridor from non-corridor pairs rather than whether it orients the corridors correctly, so direction was also scored separately: the fraction of the 24 stepping-stone links on which each estimate is larger in the imposed (forward) direction. Because `divMigrate`'s $N_m$ is itself a relative-migration statistic, rescaled within each census to its own maximum, $w_{j\mid i}$ and the true migration matrix values from the simulations were similarly standardized to homogenize comparisons.  At each census and replicate, agreement with the true matrix was summarized as Spearman's $\rho$ for both Nm and $w_{j\mid i}$.  The deviance was also evaluated using the root mean square error (RMSE).  It is important to point out that the true migration rates throughout the majority of the K(K-1)/2 entries were zero, so RMSE is dominated by agreement on non-corridor pairs rather than by how well either estimator resolves the corridors that matter—restricting to only the first-neighbor migration rates used in the simulations felts biased since the entire purpose of doing this is to try to recover the underlying patterns of connectivity, which we never have *a priori*. It is reported nonetheless for transparency rather than omitted as an unfavorable or uninformative comparison. 

# Results

### Isotropic baseline

Individual-based simulations were run to establish that the burn-in period produces stable, comparable starting topologies before any directional treatment is applied. Dyer (2007) showed that mean degree centrality ($\bar{C}_D$) and graph diameter track standard population-genetic measures of structure and gene flow ($F_{ST}$ and Slatkin's $\hat{M}$); here, where the focus is the graph's topology itself rather than that correspondence, the same two metrics serve as a check that replicate runs have converged to equilibrium. Across the isotropic replicates, $\bar{C}_D$ falls and graph diameter rises from their initial values, each relaxing toward a stable plateau within the 2,000-generation burn-in and remaining flat through the subsequent isotropic forward phase (Fig_IsotropicBaseline). The magnitude of the asymmetry index, $\overline{|\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}|}$, follows the same relaxation on the same timescale—rising from near zero toward its own plateau—consistent with it inheriting the graph's topological legacy rather than reflecting any directional gene flow, since none is present in this scenario. The signed mean, by contrast, remains centered on zero throughout the simulation (not shown; forward-phase $\bar{\Delta}$ = 0.0014, 95% CI 0.0003–0.0025), confirming that the isotropic replicates carry no net directional bias. Every replicate therefore enters the forward phase—and the redistributed and obstructed treatments that follow—from a topologically equilibrated, directionally unbiased, and directly comparable starting configuration, establishing the isotropic scenario as the empirical null against which asymmetry in the other two scenarios is judged.

### Consequences of Redistributed and Obstructed connectivity

Trends across the forward phase were estimated with a penalized regression spline rather than the parametric exponential-equilibrium curve used for the isotropic burn-in (Fig_IsotropicBaseline). The burn-in is a relaxation to a single equilibrium and the exponential form fits it well, but the forward-phase trajectories are not: redistributed diameter follows a sigmoidal decline, redistributed signed $\bar{\Delta}$ rises to a peak and then changes sign late in the window, and obstructed diameter rises before declining back toward its starting value (Fig_ScenarioComparison; Fig_SignedAsymmetry in Supplementary Materials) — no single parametric family fit all three without one or more parameters running to their bounds (e.g., a plateau parameter projected far beyond the range of the data). Each trend line is therefore a generalized additive model, $y \sim s(\text{generation}, k=20) + s(\text{replicate}, \text{bs}=\text{"re"})$, fit by REML (`mgcv`) to every replicate snapshot in the forward phase, with the per-replicate random intercept excluded from the plotted prediction; the random effect keeps a few extreme replicates from dominating the fitted trend without altering what the trend represents. The isotropic reference shown alongside each scenario is the burn-in's exponential-equilibrium fit (Fig_IsotropicBaseline), extrapolated across the forward phase.

Under the redistributed treatment, the topological signature of directional migration emerges within the forward phase itself, well before the terminal snapshot (Fig_ScenarioComparison, left). $\bar{C}_D$ and graph diameter, both flat through the isotropic forward phase (Isotropic baseline), diverge from the isotropic reference soon after treatment begins: over the final 50 snapshots (generations 2950–2999), $\bar{C}_D$ averages 0.270 against an isotropic 0.318, and diameter averages 7.90 — roughly half the isotropic 16.14 — following a sigmoidal decline. The asymmetry index tracks the same divergence: mean $|\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}|$ remains within the isotropic band through the early redistributed phase before climbing steadily to a late-phase average of 0.133 (isotropic: 0.034), roughly four-fold. This combination — rising asymmetry alongside a collapsing diameter and falling $\bar{C}_D$ — is consistent with the expectation noted in Methods that a sustained directional bias in a closed migration–drift system erodes rather than sustains a differentiated topology: redistribution is not only making gene flow asymmetric, it is progressively homogenizing the chain.

Under the obstructed treatment the asymmetry index also rises, but on this window's evidence, more slowly than under redistribution (Fig_ScenarioComparison, right). $\bar{C}_D$ declines in a similar fashion to redistributed (late-phase average 0.259 against isotropic 0.318). Graph diameter behaves qualitatively differently from redistributed, rather than tracing a delayed version of the same shape: redistributed diameter stays near the isotropic level until about generation 2350, then collapses sharply to roughly 7, while obstructed diameter instead rises substantially — to roughly 19.5 — around generation 2500–2750, holds near that peak, and only eases slightly after generation 2800 (late-phase average 18.37 against isotropic 16.14, still declining at generation 3000). The two trajectories differ in kind, not merely in timescale; it is the signed asymmetry index, not diameter, that shares a common shape across both treatments. The signed graph mean $\bar{\Delta}$ is positive in both scenarios through reorganization—upstream populations reading as sources, the imposed direction—and the time each takes to reach its maximum differs two-fold (Supplementary Materials, Fig_SignedAsymmetry): redistributed $\bar{\Delta}$ peaks around generation 2435 (~400 generations post-onset), and obstructed $\bar{\Delta}$ around generation 2820 (~800 generations post-onset), after which it eases only slightly and stays positive through the end of the window. Redistributed $\bar{\Delta}$ then changes sign late in the window (median generation 2684 across the 39 lineages in which it crosses zero). This is not a reversal of direction. The population-level source–sink score keeps the imposed ordering through it—$r(S,x)<0$ in 97% of redistributed censuses in generations 2504–2749—and fades only as lineages collapse (Significance Testing & False Positive Rates). The sign change is carried by the interior of the graph rather than its ends: in 92% of the crossing lineages it coincides with the sign change of the sum over interior edges, and in none with that of the chain-end terms. Because $\bar{\Delta}$ weights each population's score by the balance of its edges pointing up versus down the chain, rewiring of the interior graph as lineages collapse can change the sign of $\bar{\Delta}$ while the ordering of sources and sinks along the chain holds (Supplementary Materials, *Decomposition of the late sign change in $\bar{\Delta}$*). The two-fold difference in time to peak directional signal tracks the difference in net directional migration bias between the scenarios exactly: $m_{i\rightarrow i+1}-m_{i\leftarrow i+1}=0.03$ for redistributed versus $0.015$ for obstructed (Methods; each scenario was designed to isolate one departure from isotropic rather than to hold total migration rate fixed against the other, so total flux also differs between them—0.05 versus 0.035—and is not excluded as a contributor, though its smaller, roughly 1.4-fold difference tracks the observed timescale ratio less closely than $\Delta m$ does). Mean $|\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}|$ is consistent with the same picture, rising more gradually than under redistribution to a late-phase average of 0.062, roughly half the redistributed value. The asymmetry index and diameter therefore diverge in what they show about obstructed's progress toward homogenization within this window: $|\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}|$ and the signed asymmetry index both trace slowed, roughly twice-as-slow versions of redistributed's trajectory, but graph diameter instead rises and holds near a new plateau rather than collapsing—==perhaps because the smaller net bias and reduced total flux under obstruction are enough to reshape the graph's connectivity pattern without yet driving the chain toward the single, fully homogenized topology that redistribution reaches within this window==; the 1,000-generation forward window may simply not be long enough to see obstructed's diameter collapse the way redistributed's does.

### Directional isolation by graph distance

The same reorganization–peak–collapse pattern is visible in the graph's principal downstream use: relating conditional genetic distance to geographic distance as a measure of isolation by graph distance (IBGD). Standard IBGD—the fit ($R^2$) of symmetric cGD regressed against pairwise spatial separation, $|dx|$—stays flat under isotropic migration (medians of 0.79–0.81 across the forward phase) but weakens once migration becomes asymmetric, falling to roughly 0.30–0.35 by generation 3000 under redistribution and more gradually to roughly 0.63–0.64 under obstruction (Fig_DirectionalIBGD, top). Part of what standard IBGD loses under asymmetry is directional structure that a model allowing pGD separate forward and reverse slopes against the same spatial distance can recover: the $R^2$ gained by modelling direction explicitly—a direction-aware pGD fit minus a direction-blind pGD fit against the same $|dx|$—sits at a floor of about 0.03 under isotropic migration (not exactly zero, since splitting each edge into two directional observations always lets the second slope absorb some noise, together with each lineage's own small burn-in lean; Isotropic baseline), rises to a peak of about 0.27–0.29 during redistributed reorganization (generations 2400–2700) before falling back as those lineages collapse, and rises more slowly under obstruction to about 0.23–0.25, still near that level at generation 2999 without having collapsed within the window—consistent with obstruction's smaller net directional bias and lower total migration rate moving every phase more slowly (Consequences of Redistributed and Obstructed connectivity; Methods).

### Significance Testing & False Positive Rates

#### Source–sink gradient test

Under symmetric migration the source–sink gradient test rejected at close to its nominal rate (Tab_SourceSinkCalibration). At $\alpha=0.05$ it rejected in 4.2% of burn-in censuses and 6.0% of isotropic forward-phase censuses, and across the symmetric differentiation gradient in 3.6–5.8%, with no increase as neutral differentiation rose (sym-mid 5.1%, sym-low 5.8%, sym-verylow 3.6%; Supplementary Materials, *Differentiation without Direction*). The p-values were close to uniform (burn-in: 8–12% of censuses in each tenth of the unit interval). The null is what makes this possible. Permuting $S$ across populations while ignoring the graph rejected in 24% of burn-in censuses, and Moran spectral randomization on the true landscape adjacency—the 24-link chain that generated the data—still rejected in 11%. Spectral randomization on the census's own Population Graph did better than either, because $S$ inherits its smoothness from the graph's topology, including its non-adjacent edges, not from the chain; the Population Graph is therefore the better basis for a spatial null than the landscape itself.

Averaged over each lineage's censuses, $r(S,x)$ is positive in 36 of the 50 burn-in lineages (sign test $p=0.003$), and every symmetric scenario inherits the lean because all of them branch from those same 50 lineages: 36 of 50 in the isotropic forward phase ($p=0.003$), and 31, 33 and 35 of 50 in sym-mid, sym-low and sym-verylow. The lean places sources slightly downstream, opposite to the direction imposed in the asymmetric treatments, so it works against detection there rather than inflating it. It is also the source of the small negative isotropic $\bar{\Delta}$ (Isotropic baseline). The graph construction does not produce it: rebuilding the Population Graphs after mirroring the deme labels reverses $r(S,x)$ exactly, and one simulated generation of migration and mating from the saved burn-in states shows no upstream or downstream preference in the parents of interior demes (Supplementary Materials, *Differentiation without Direction*).

#### Directional isolation by graph distance

Under symmetric migration the directional isolation model almost never displaces standard IBGD. In the final 100 generations of burn-in, $R^2_{pGD}$ exceeded $R^2_{cGD}$ in 1.9% of censuses (19 of 1,000; 95% CI 1.1–3.0%), and across the entire isotropic forward phase in 4.1% (95% CI 3.7–4.5%, 10,000 censuses), with a median $\Delta R^2$ of $-0.055$ in both. Without any calibration to the simulations, the decision rule $\Delta R^2>0$ therefore holds a false-positive rate at or below a conventional 5% level. The negative median is the cost of the directional description when there is no direction to describe: splitting each edge into two directional observations adds scatter that the forward and reverse slopes cannot recover (Directional isolation by graph distance).

Under asymmetric migration the comparison reverses, tracking the phases of the forward simulation (Tab_IBGDDetection, Supplementary Materials). During reorganization (generations 2004–2249) the directional model was preferred in only 12% of redistributed and 6% of obstructed censuses, barely above the symmetric rate—the imposed asymmetry has not yet restructured the graph. Once it has, detection rose to 54–73% of redistributed censuses, peaking at 73% in generations 2504–2749 before declining to 55% as lineages collapsed. Obstructed connectivity, with its smaller net directional bias and lower total migration, moved through the same sequence more slowly, rising from 20% (2254–2499) to 43% and then 54% by the end of the window, without collapsing. Where the directional model was preferred, it also recovered the imposed direction: the forward-to-reverse slope ratio was below one in 99% of detections under both treatments (median 0.31 redistributed, 0.37 obstructed), against a median of 1.11 (IQR 0.87–1.40) under isotropic migration.

The test is one-sided in what it can establish. A preferred directional fit is good evidence of asymmetric gene flow along the hypothesized axis, but a preferred symmetric fit is not evidence against it: even at their peaks, 27–46% of asymmetric censuses still favored cGD, and during reorganization nearly all did. Two alternatives were considered and rejected as the decision statistic. The gain from adding the direction term within pGD alone (direction-aware against direction-blind pGD) is positive in 94% of isotropic censuses, because the added slope always absorbs some noise, so it can serve as a test only with a threshold calibrated by simulation. The slope ratio separates the scenarios more sharply (AUC 0.92–0.99 against isotropic once asymmetry is established), but its detection threshold is likewise simulation-specific; it is reported here as the description of a detected asymmetry rather than as the test for one.

The comparison also bears on what each representation of the graph is for. For isolation by distance in the ordinary sense—genetic distance against the separation of sampling locations, with no hypothesis about direction—cGD is the appropriate distance, and splitting it directionally only adds noise. When the migration process is itself directional, however, the directional partition of the same edges describes isolation better than the symmetric one, and it does so specifically when, and because, gene flow is asymmetric. This is the downstream counterpart of the fidelity result (Comparison to `divMigrate`): the directional weights recover the imposed migration matrix, and that information is what the directional isolation model exploits.



### Divergence among redistributed lineages

Because each replicate evolves as an independent stochastic realization once asymmetric migration is imposed, individual redistributed lineages are not expected to track a single shared trajectory (Methods). Two lines of evidence bear on this directly. First, redistributed lineages lose genetic diversity at markedly different rates: the summed effective number of alleles across loci, $\sum A_e$, diverges from the isotropic band over the course of the forward phase, and several replicates approach fixation well before others (Fig_LineageDiversity). Re-indexing asymmetry against each replicate's own remaining diversity rather than generation collapses much of this spread — mean $|\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}|$ and signed $\bar\Delta$ both stay within the isotropic band until $\sum A_e$ falls below roughly 25–27, then rise steeply as replicates approach fixation (Fig_LineageClock), consistent with the asymmetry signal tracking how far each lineage has progressed toward fixation rather than elapsed time alone. A companion comparison against a null built from information loss alone — rebuilding isotropic graphs from random subsets of polymorphic loci — shows that information loss inflates $|\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}|$ by more than 10% only below an informative horizon of about 4.5 polymorphic loci per deme (Fig_InformationNull), so the late-phase rise in asymmetry is not simply an artifact of shrinking locus counts, though it does contribute once replicates approach that horizon.

### Bandwidth sensitivity

The bandwidth estimator $b_{i}=s_{i}/k_{i}$ is a modeling choice (Methods); the question here is not its absolute scale but whether the *sign* and *rank order* of $\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$—which edge flows which way, and which edges carry the strongest signal—survive a deliberately non-local choice. $\Delta$ was recomputed under each of the alternative bandwidths (Methods) and compared, edge by edge, to the canonical local estimator on the comparable $w_{i\mid j}-w_{j\mid i}$ scale: the fraction of edges with the same sign, the Spearman correlation of the signed $\Delta$ and of $|\Delta |$, and whether the graph-mean $\bar{\Delta}$ keeps its sign, all recorded against the canonical index over the directional scenarios.

The result separates the aggregate from the edge level (Tab_BandwidthSensitivity). Edge-level direction is only *moderately* preserved: across the four non-local alternatives, the sign of $\Delta_{i\rightarrow j}$ agrees with the canonical local estimator on 75–84% of edges—so roughly one edge in five changes orientation—and the signed rank order correlates at Spearman $\rho =0.38$–0.78, weakest for the perplexity kernel (a genuinely different construction rather than a rescaling) and imperfect even for the mild $\times \frac{1}{2}$ and $\times 2$ rescalings. What these numbers mean for how edge-level versus aggregate results should be read is taken up in Discussion (Caveats and limitations).

### Comparison to `divMigrate`

Across all three scenarios, the pGD-derived similarity $\sigma_{ij}$ and divMigrate's $N_m$ track a common signal for most of the forward phase before diverging, rather than disagreeing from the outset (Fig_DivMigrateCorrelation). Under isotropic migration the two remain in close agreement throughout, with Spearman's $\rho$ fluctuating narrowly around 0.80–0.85. Under redistributed connectivity, $\rho$ starts near this isotropic level (\~0.83) and declines steadily to roughly 0.60 by generation 3000, with the spread across replicates widening sharply from around generation 2700–2800 onward — coincident with the onset of census-to-census instability in $\sigma_{ij}$ under this scenario (Supplementary Materials). Under obstructed connectivity the decline is shallower and more gradual, from roughly 0.80 to 0.70, with less replicate-to-replicate spread than under redistribution throughout.

![](media/fig-divmig-correlation-all.png)
*Fig_DivMigrateCorrelation:* Similarity in the evolution of asymmetry between the pGD-derived similarity matrix $\sigma_{ij}$ and divMigrate's $N_m$-based relative migration matrix (Similarity in the evolution of asymmetry), by treatment (panels): Spearman's $\rho$ between the two matrices' off-diagonal entries at each census, for every replicate (grey) with the across-replicate median (red). Under isotropic migration $\rho$ is flat throughout the forward phase, fluctuating narrowly around 0.80-0.85. Under redistribution $\rho$ starts near the isotropic level (\~0.83) and declines steadily to a median of roughly 0.60 by generation 3000, with the spread across replicates widening sharply from around generation 2700-2800 onward — well after diameter has already collapsed (Consequences of Redistributed and Obstructed connectivity) but coincident with the onset of the lag-1 instability in $\sigma$ (Supplementary Materials, Fig_DivMigrateLag1). Under obstruction the decline is shallower and more gradual, from \~0.80 to \~0.70, with less replicate-to-replicate spread than redistribution throughout. In both asymmetric scenarios the two independently derived measures of directional structure track one another closely for most of the forward phase before diverging, rather than disagreeing from the outset.

Because the forward-phase simulations impose a known, fixed migration schedule, the true directional migration matrix is available independently of any inference drawn from the genotypes themselves — a comparison possible here only because the underlying process was simulated rather than observed, and one with no analog when applying either method to empirical data. Against this ground truth, genetic gravity's raw neighbourhood weight $w_{j\mid i}$, compared with the transposed truth $m_{j\rightarrow i}$ (Methods), tracks the true matrix more closely than divMigrate's $N_m$ under isotropic and obstructed migration throughout the forward phase (Fig_DivMigrateTruth): median Spearman's $\rho$ is 0.53 against 0.45 under isotropic migration and 0.52 against 0.43 under obstruction, and $w_{j\mid i}$ has the higher $\rho$ in more than 99% of censuses at which both are defined. Under redistributed migration the same advantage holds until the topology itself begins to destabilize late in the window. The advantage lies in identifying *which* pairs exchange migrants rather than in orienting them. Transposing the truth matrix changes either estimator's median agreement by no more than 0.001, because over all ordered pairs $\rho$ is dominated by the separation of the 48 true corridors from the 552 pairs with no direct migration, which the Population Graph's sparse topology captures well. Scored on direction alone, $N_m$ does better: along the 24 true stepping-stone links it is larger in the imposed direction on 60–66% of links during and after reorganization, against 53–56% for $w_{j\mid i}$, while both sit at 49–50% under isotropic migration. The weak orientation of individual adjacent links is consistent with the directional signal in the graph being carried mostly by the longer, non-adjacent edges and read most reliably at the population level (Significance Testing & False Positive Rates). Towards the end of the forward phase, loci at affected pairs become fixed and `divMigrate`'s $N_m$ turns undefined or spuriously negative rather than simply small (Fig_DivMigrateTimecourse); any census carrying such a value on any of the $K(K-1)$ pairs — not only retained graph edges — was excluded from the paired comparison entirely, for both `divMigrate` and its own rescaling denominator, rather than clipped or substituted. This exclusion is most severe in late-window redistributed censuses, where $N_m$'s own comparison ends up resting on an increasingly small and unrepresentative subset of replicates in which it remains defined (only 4% of redistributed replicates retained at generation 2999, and 0–8% over the final 50 generations). To avoid crediting $w_{j\mid i}$ with an advantage that is partly an artifact of that shrinking, easier subset, its agreement with the truth is also reported across every census regardless of $N_m$'s availability, alongside the percentage of replicates retained at each census; on that fairer, all-censuses basis, $w_{j\mid i}$'s own agreement with the truth also declines late in the redistributed window, to roughly 0.14–0.22 over the final 25 generations, below $N_m$'s 0.29–0.36 on its few remaining replicates, coinciding with the same loss of topological structure evident in graph diameter and in census-to-census stability (Consequences of Redistributed and Obstructed connectivity; Supplementary Materials). Spearman's $\rho$ is the primary measure of agreement reported here; RMSE was also examined but discriminates less cleanly between the two estimators and is reported alongside it for completeness (Methods).
