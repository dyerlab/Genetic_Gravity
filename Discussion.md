# Discussion

## The contribution and its scope

Genetic gravity converts the undirected skeleton of a Population Graph into a signed field of directional influence using only the conditional edge weights the graph already produces, with no demographic model, no equilibrium assumption, no spatial layer, and no free tuning parameter. The preceding sections establish what the asymmetry index certifies from a single snapshot: it is specific, sitting near zero under symmetric migration however high neutral differentiation rises; its directional content is robust at the network level though bandwidth-sensitive at the level of the individual edge; and it avoids the drift-driven false positives and fixation artifacts of pairwise differentiation-based estimators. The complementary question of how these directional signals *accumulate over time*—the deep-time trajectory of $\bar{\Delta}$ and the restructuring of the graph’s spectral and path-based metrics under sustained asymmetric connectivity—is the subject of a companion paper. 

In the isotropic burn-in, $\overline{|\Delta_{ij}|}$ itself rises well above zero even with no directional gene flow present (Results); this reflects the graph's topological legacy from a finite burn-in rather than any real asymmetry, which is why the signed $\bar{\Delta}$, not $\overline{|\Delta_{ij}|}$, is the diagnostic quantity for directionality, and why every scenario is judged against its own empirical isotropic reference rather than an algebraic zero (Methods). The redistributed and obstructed simulations also test two specific theoretical expectations set out in Methods. First, that a sustained directional migration bias in a closed migration–drift system erodes rather than sustains a differentiated topology: under redistribution, rising asymmetry is accompanied by a collapsing graph diameter and falling $\bar{C}_D$ (Results), consistent with the chain being progressively homogenized rather than merely reshuffled. Second, that the relative difference between forward and reverse migration rates, $\Delta m$, rather than their combined total flux, should govern how strongly two asymmetric regimes are expected to differ. Both the magnitude of the interior asymmetry index and the speed with which it becomes detectable track the two-fold difference in $\Delta m$ between redistributed and obstructed more closely than they track the smaller, 1.4-fold difference in total flux between the same two scenarios (Results), supporting $\Delta m$ as the better predictor of cross-scenario strength.

## Relationship to existing methods

The directional methods surveyed in the Introduction each occupy a different point on the trade-off between demographic interpretability and assumption load. Assignment-based estimators of recent migration (Wilson & Rannala, 2003) and coalescent or site-frequency spectrum approaches (Beerli & Felsenstein, 2001; Gutenkunst et al., 2009) return demographically interpretable, asymmetric migration rates, but at the cost of equilibrium or explicit-model assumptions, genome-scale data or few populations, and substantial computational cost. Resistance-surface and effective-migration-surface methods (Daniel et al., 2024; Marcus et al., 2021; Petkova et al., 2016) recover landscape-level effective gene flow but require spatial coordinates and, in their classical form, treat migration as symmetric. The expansion directionality index $\psi$ (Peter & Slatkin, 2013) is closest in spirit—reading direction from allele-frequency asymmetry—but is tailored to a single range-expansion origin rather than to an arbitrary connectivity topology. Genetic gravity complements all of these: a topology-derived, coordinate-free index of directional influence that requires no demographic model, no equilibrium assumption, and no spatial layer, that can be computed from a single snapshot, and that scales trivially with sample and population size. It is not a replacement for migration-rate estimation; it is a prior structural summary that narrows the hypothesis space for those heavier methods.

The practical implication is that genetic gravity is the more specific screen for *whether* directionality is present, and the differentiation- and model-based estimators are the appropriate follow-ups for quantifying *how much* and *at what rate*, once it is present.

This contrast is, at root, about *network conditioning*. divMigrate, like any pairwise estimator, evaluates each dyad in isolation: the relative migration assigned to $(i,j)$ is computed from the differentiation of $i$ and $j$ alone, with no reference to the rest of the network. An indirect channel—gene flow from $A$ to $C$ routed through an intermediate $B$—therefore registers as direct $A$–$C$ differentiation, and across a landscape these indirect signals accumulate into a dense field of spurious directional edges that an analyst cannot easily separate from the genuine ones. Genetic gravity begins from a different object. A Population Graph is a Markov random field in which every retained edge has already been conditioned on the rest of the network, and conditionally independent pairs—precisely the indirect $A$–$C$ channels—are pruned before any direction is assigned; the kernel then reads direction only off edges that survived that conditioning. The conditional-independence structure thus acts as a structural filter against false-positive directional edges: the framework cannot manufacture an apparent $A\rightarrow C$ flow that is in fact $A\rightarrow B\rightarrow C$, because the $A$–$C$ edge does not exist in the graph to be directed. This is a categorical rather than incremental advantage—no degree of care in a pairwise method recovers the global conditioning the Population Graph performs by construction—and it is the deeper reason the asymmetry index stays near zero under symmetric migration where a differentiation-based estimator cannot.

The biological interpretation of $\Delta_{ij}$ is straightforward when the framework’s assumptions align with the system. Positive $\Delta_{ij}$ identifies edges where population $i$ is overwriting the conditional allele-frequency structure of population $j$—a signature of range expansion, source-sink dynamics, or asymmetric vector-mediated dispersal. More broadly, the emergent distinction between “extended” edges (high $w_{i\rightarrow j}$ at long $e_{ij}$; a putative corridor or expansion front) and “compressed” edges (low $w_{i\rightarrow j}$ at short $e_{ij}$; a putative vicariance or dispersal barrier) connects the framework directly to the evolutionary processes cataloged in R. Dyer (2007).

## Scope and reliability: matching the tool to the question

In the spirit of Box’s dictum that all models are wrong but some are useful, the genetic-gravity kernel is plainly an idealization—it imposes a Gaussian decay and a particular local bandwidth that the biology never guarantees—and the purpose of the simulations was less to vindicate it than to bench-test where it is useful and where it is not. The clearest result to emerge is a separation by *scale of inference*: the framework is robust at the aggregate, network level, and fragile at the level of the individual edge. The graph-mean asymmetry index $\bar{\Delta}$ and the longitudinal graph-metric trajectories reliably and specifically recover the presence, mechanism, and timing of asymmetric connectivity, and the aggregate orientation survives bandwidth changes that reshuffle individual edges. Individual-edge orientations, by contrast, are sensitive to the bandwidth choice (roughly one edge in five changes sign under a non-local bandwidth), carry a deterministic degree component, are not independent observations, and have no calibrated significance test—every null tried against a single graph's own edges was anti-conservative (Supplementary Materials, Permutation nulls for $\Delta_{ij}$); they are therefore best read as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. One caveat applies even to the aggregate signal: because $|\bar{\Delta}|$ is the mean of a quantity centered near zero, it creeps upward as per-edge noise increases under high neutral differentiation (Results)—modestly, and far below the level the asymmetric scenarios reach, but enough that a rise in $|\bar{\Delta}|$ alone should be read alongside the signed $\bar{\Delta}$ before being attributed to directional gene flow.

This separation organizes the inferential targets into the hierarchy in Tab_ToolSummary (Supplementary Materials) and tells a practitioner which tool certifies what. At the network level, the question "is there directional structure, and of what kind?" is answered by $\bar{\Delta}$ and the graph metrics against the empirical isotropic null—the most reliable use of the framework, though it needs replicates or a time series to build that null. Where only a single Population Graph is available, the directional-vs-symmetric IBGD model comparison (Methods, Significance Testing for Asymmetry) answers the same presence question at the level of the whole graph, with an empirically characterized false-positive rate (1.9-4.1% under symmetric migration; Results) and no replicates required—at the cost of being one-sided (a preferred symmetric fit is not evidence against asymmetry) and unable to say which edges carry the signal. Edge-level scanning was tried and abandoned: neither a topology-rewiring null nor any permutation or sign-exchangeability null built from a single graph's own parts is exchangeable, because $\Delta_{ij}$ carries node-level structure and a drift-generated, spatially smooth potential that none of them reproduce (Supplementary Materials, Permutation nulls for $\Delta_{ij}$); individual-edge $\Delta_{ij}$ therefore has no calibrated test and is read as exploratory only. And the contrast with divMigrate positions genetic gravity among differentiation-based estimators: concordant where directional flow is genuinely present, more specific under symmetry, and structurally insulated from the fixation-driven normalization blow-up that destabilizes differentiation-magnitude methods.

The contribution, then, is not a single index but a suite of complementary tools with an explicit reliability envelope around each. Stating those envelopes honestly—what is robust, what is exploratory, and which null answers which question—is what allows the framework to be applied responsibly: a practitioner who treats $\bar{\Delta}$ and the graph-level trajectories as load-bearing while reading individual-edge arrows as leads for further work, and who matches the significance test to the sampling design, can extract directional inference from undirected Population Graphs without overreaching what the data support.



The entire reason the graphical-Gaussian-model / precision-matrix approach in Population Graphs exists is to solve exactly this problem: a raw pairwise distance (or a pairwise `divMigrate` estimate) can't distinguish a direct relationship between two populations from an indirect one mediated through a third. Two populations that never exchange migrants but both receive alleles from a common intermediate will show up as "related" in any purely dyadic measure, because nothing in a pairwise calculation can partial out the intermediate's contribution. The Population Graph's edges are conditional on the whole topology precisely so that spurious indirect relationships collapse to non-edges — that's the theoretical payoff of the graphical model over a plain distance matrix and what motivated Dyer & Nason (2004).  `divMigrate` has no way to do that. It takes two populations, estimates a statistic from just their genotypes, and repeats for every pair.  It isn't a simplification that happens to be there; pairwise approaches are structurally incapable of representing anything beyond a two-population system, so they cannot differentiate a real direct signal from relatedness that's actually propagating through the rest of the connectivity network.

## Caveats and limitations

Several caveats apply. First, the bandwidth estimator $b_{i}=s_{i}/k_{i}$ is a modeling choice, though a constrained one, defended on the three grounds set out in the Methods (parameter-free, scale-equivariant, and singled out by the graph’s Markov structure). Whether the *sign* and rank order of $\Delta_{ij}$ are preserved under deliberately non-local alternatives—a fixed global bandwidth, a locally rescaled bandwidth, or a perplexity-based per-node bandwidth—was tested directly (Results; Tab_BandwidthSensitivity), and the answer is split by scale: edge-level direction is only *moderately* preserved, with the sign of $\Delta_{ij}$ agreeing with the canonical estimator on 75–84% of edges (roughly one edge in five reorienting) and signed rank order correlating at Spearman $\rho=0.38$–0.78, weakest for the perplexity kernel; the graph-mean $\bar{\Delta}$ is steadier, its sign preserved across alternatives in 87% of redistributed and 84% of obstructed snapshots. The bandwidth is therefore a genuine modeling choice at the edge level rather than an immaterial convenience; what is robust is the aggregate directional signal, which is what the conclusions in this manuscript rest on, while individual-edge $\Delta_{ij}$ should be read as exploratory, consistent with the edge non-independence and degree dependence discussed below. The local estimator remains the principled default on the grounds above, not on edge-level invariance.

Second, because the directional weights are normalized within each node’s neighborhood, the asymmetry index carries a purely topological component that is largest at the periphery of the graph. A degree-one node—a chain endpoint, or any population retained with a single neighbor—places its entire kernel mass on that one neighbor, so $w_{i\rightarrow j}=1$ irrespective of bandwidth or migration direction; the edge incident to such a node then registers a large $|\Delta_{ij}|$ that reflects graph geometry rather than directional gene flow. More generally, whenever two endpoints differ in degree or neighborhood composition, their directional weights differ even under perfectly symmetric migration. This is why $\bar{\Delta}$ is calibrated against the empirical isotropic null rather than an algebraic zero (Methods), and why edge-level $\Delta_{ij}$ values at low-degree nodes should be interpreted cautiously. I examine the boundary contribution directly in Supplementary Materials (Pendants, boundary effects, and other marginalia issues relevant to applying $\Delta_{ij}$), decomposing the signal into leaf-incident and interior edges and showing that scenario discrimination is an interior property. Consistent with this, no leaf-incident edges occurred under the isotropic scenario in this dataset at any sampled generation (Supplementary Materials)—degree-one termini appear to be themselves a signature of the asymmetric regimes rather than a scenario-independent geometric baseline, reinforcing the point below that the leaf-node behavior of $\Delta_{ij}$ tracks a genuine evolutionary regime rather than only a numerical edge case. That boundary contribution is not merely a nuisance to be calibrated away, however: a terminus deme exchanges with a single neighbor and so receives roughly half the immigrant input of an interior deme, tipping its drift–migration balance toward drift and accelerating the loss of the variation the graph is built from (Supplementary Materials) — the same process that, seen at the periphery, drives divMigrate's $N_m$ estimator to break down at late generations — because $N_m$ is a ratio built on pairwise differentiation, a pair fixed for the same allele at nearly every locus drives both numerator and denominator toward zero together and leaves the estimate undefined, precisely once drift has run polymorphism down — while the topology-derived index keeps reading the conditional structure that remains. The leaf-node behavior of $\Delta_{ij}$ is thus a marker of a genuine evolutionary regime at a stepping-stone terminus — weak immigration, strong drift, and an unambiguous source- or sink-terminus polarity — not only a geometric artifact; a principled correction—for instance, conditioning the null on node degree—is a natural refinement left to future work.

Third, the local kernel only reads directionality off edges that already exist in the topology; populations that are conditionally independent in the Population Graph receive zero directional weight by construction. The method therefore inherits the edge-retention properties of the underlying Population Graph estimator, and directional inference is only as good as the topology it rides on. Fourth, $\Delta_{ij}$ reflects the *structural* influence of one population on another, not a demographic migration rate; inferring $m$ from $\Delta$ requires an additional model and is outside the scope of this work. Finally, the validation simulations omit mutation, so allelic diversity decays slowly under drift across the 3,000-generation horizon; this isolates the drift–migration processes the framework is designed to detect, but it bounds the window over which the metapopulation retains the polymorphism needed to estimate conditional covariance, and a mutation–drift treatment would be required to extend the analysis to longer timescales. Two specific findings in Results are limited by this same window. Under obstructed connectivity, graph diameter rises and holds near a new plateau rather than collapsing the way it does under redistribution (Results); whether this reflects a genuinely different long-run endpoint—perhaps because the smaller net bias and reduced total flux under obstruction are enough to reshape the graph's connectivity pattern without yet driving it toward the single, fully homogenized topology redistribution reaches within this window—or simply a slower approach to the same eventual collapse that a 1,000-generation forward window is too short to observe, cannot be distinguished with the current simulations. Similarly, the apparent decline in $\overline{|\Delta_{ij}|}$ after each redistributed replicate's own peak (Fig_PeakAlignment) is not yet distinguishable from the noise a stable trajectory produces over the same interval—the redistributed decline (13%) and the isotropic control's decline over an equivalent window (17%) are comparable in relative size—and the peaks themselves fall close to where information loss begins to inflate the index (Results), so part of the pattern may reflect the last informative loci fixing rather than the underlying covariance settling toward equilibrium. Extending the redistributed and obstructed simulations past generation 3000, or repeating them with mutation, would let both questions be resolved directly.

#### The ‘why do downtowns always have 1-way streets’ correlary

==Since $p_{ij}\leq e_{ij}$ for every edge and every direction, every directed arc in the pGD graph is weighted at most as much as the corresponding symmetric cGD edge. Shortest-path distance is monotonic non-decreasing in edge weights — reduce any edge weight and no shortest path can get longer, only shorter or unchanged. So for every ordered pair  $(i,j)$, $D_{ij}$ computed on the pGD graph is $\leq$ what one would get running shortest paths on the plain symmetric cGD graph, and therefore the sum over all pairs is smaller too. Under full symmetry that reduction is exact and uniform — every edge is scaled by exactly $1/2$, so the total sum of all shortest paths is exactly half the cGD total, no exceptions.==

==Under real asymmetry, though, the reduction stops being uniform. As $\Delta_{ij}$ grows at a given edge, one direction's share drifts toward $e_{ij}$ (nearly the full weight) while the other drifts toward zero. A path that happens to route "with the grain" of the dominant flow direction (this is where i get the synergy with street planning for downtown areas) gets progressively cheaper as asymmetry increases; a path running against it stays close to the full cGD cost. So the total isn't just smaller than the symmetric case, it becomes increasingly *unevenly* smaller — concentrated specifically on pairs whose shortest route happens to align with wherever the strong asymmetric edges point.  And for the analogy, this is why all the important businesses are also, not coincidentlally on the 1-way streets in downtown—they are non-liearly closer and it is ‘faster’ to get to them.==

==That has a direct bearing on the homogenization story. Part of what would look like "downstream recipients homogenizing" in a falling $S_{ij}$-vs-$M$ correlation could be genuine biology — or it could be this mechanical property of the pGD partition itself: as asymmetry increases across the topology, with-the-grain pairs get systematically tighter distances by construction, independent of whether their underlying cGD has moved at all. Both effects push in the same direction at the same time, which makes them hard to tell apart from the correlation trend alone. Worth thinking about whether there's a way to isolate the two — for instance, whether cGD itself is also compressing for those same downstream pairs (which would support real homogenization) or whether it's holding roughly steady while only the directional partition of it swings (which would point at the construction effect instead).==

## Extensions

Several extensions follow naturally. Landscape-conditioned genetic gravity—in which the kernel bandwidth $b_{i}$ is modulated by at-site ecological covariates, after the gravity-model treatment of pollen flow in Dileo et al. (2014)—should improve the biological specificity of the directional signal. Temporal sampling, either by resampling the same populations across years or by using time-structured genotypes, converts the static $\Delta_{ij}$ into a series whose derivative directly encodes the expansion rate. And the directional-vs-symmetric IBGD comparison introduced above generalizes to node-restricted comparisons (e.g., restricting the comparison to edges incident to a single node) that support hypothesis tests about individual populations acting as sources or sinks.

Taken together, these results argue that directionality is recoverable from the information already present in a Population Graph, without introducing new modeling machinery, and that the asymmetry index $\Delta_{ij}$—together with the directed-graph metrics and $\bar{\Delta}$ it generates—provides a useful, interpretable summary of that directional information. The approach is implemented in the gstudio R package and is ready for use on existing data.
