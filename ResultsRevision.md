## The Evolution of asymmetry in genetic topologies

### Isotropic baseline

Individual-based simulations were run to establish that the burn-in period produces stable, comparable starting topologies before any directional treatment is applied. Dyer (2007) showed that mean degree centrality ($\bar{C}_D$) and graph diameter track standard population-genetic measures of structure and gene flow ($F_{ST}$ and Slatkin's $\hat{M}$); here, where the focus is the graph's topology itself rather than that correspondence, the same two metrics serve as a check that replicate runs have converged to equilibrium. Across the isotropic replicates, $\bar{C}_D$ falls and graph diameter rises from their initial values, each relaxing toward a stable plateau within the 2,000-generation burn-in and remaining flat through the subsequent isotropic forward phase (Fig_IsotropicBaseline). The magnitude of the asymmetry index, $\overline{|\Delta_{ij}|}$, follows the same relaxation on the same timescale—rising from near zero toward its own plateau—consistent with it inheriting the graph's topological legacy rather than reflecting any directional gene flow, since none is present in this scenario. The signed mean, by contrast, remains centered on zero throughout the simulation (not shown; forward-phase $\bar{\Delta}$ = 0.0014, 95% CI 0.0003–0.0025), confirming that the isotropic replicates carry no net directional bias. Every replicate therefore enters the forward phase—and the redistributed and obstructed treatments that follow—from a topologically equilibrated, directionally unbiased, and directly comparable starting configuration, establishing the isotropic scenario as the empirical null against which asymmetry in the other two scenarios is judged.

### Asymmetry of redistributed and obstructed connectivity

Trends across the forward phase were estimated with a penalized regression spline rather than the parametric exponential-equilibrium curve used for the isotropic burn-in (Fig_IsotropicBaseline). The burn-in is a relaxation to a single equilibrium and the exponential form fits it well, but the forward-phase trajectories are not: redistributed diameter follows a sigmoidal decline, redistributed signed $\bar{\Delta}$ is biphasic (negative before reversing to positive), and obstructed diameter rises before declining back toward its starting value (Fig_Redistributed, Fig_Obstructed, and Fig_SignedAsymmetry in Supplementary Materials) — no single parametric family fit all three without one or more parameters running to their bounds (e.g., a plateau parameter projected far beyond the range of the data). Each trend line is therefore a generalized additive model, $y \sim s(\text{generation}, k=20) + s(\text{replicate}, \text{bs}=\text{"re"})$, fit by REML (`mgcv`) to every replicate snapshot in the forward phase, with the per-replicate random intercept excluded from the plotted prediction; the random effect keeps a few extreme replicates from dominating the fitted trend without altering what the trend represents. The isotropic reference shown alongside each scenario is the burn-in's exponential-equilibrium fit (Fig_IsotropicBaseline), extrapolated across the forward phase.

Under the redistributed treatment, the topological signature of directional migration emerges within the forward phase itself, well before the terminal snapshot (Fig_Redistributed). $\bar{C}_D$ and graph diameter, both flat through the isotropic forward phase (Isotropic baseline), diverge from the isotropic reference soon after treatment begins: over the final 50 snapshots (generations 2950–2999), $\bar{C}_D$ averages 0.270 against an isotropic 0.318, and diameter averages 7.90 — roughly half the isotropic 16.14 — following a sigmoidal decline. The asymmetry index tracks the same divergence: mean $|\Delta_{ij}|$ remains within the isotropic band through the early redistributed phase before climbing steadily to a late-phase average of 0.133 (isotropic: 0.034), roughly four-fold, consistent with the terminal-generation value reported in Tab_GravityVsDivMigrate. This combination — rising asymmetry alongside a collapsing diameter and falling $\bar{C}_D$ — is consistent with the expectation noted in Methods that a sustained directional bias in a closed migration–drift system erodes rather than sustains a differentiated topology: redistribution is not only making gene flow asymmetric, it is progressively homogenizing the chain.

Under the obstructed treatment the asymmetry index also rises, but on this window's evidence, more slowly than under redistribution (Fig_Obstructed). $\bar{C}_D$ declines in a similar fashion to redistributed (late-phase average 0.259 against isotropic 0.318). Graph diameter behaves qualitatively differently from redistributed, rather than tracing a delayed version of the same shape: redistributed diameter stays near the isotropic level until about generation 2350, then collapses sharply to roughly 7, while obstructed diameter instead rises substantially — to roughly 19.5 — around generation 2500–2750, holds near that peak, and only eases slightly after generation 2800 (late-phase average 18.37 against isotropic 16.14, still declining at generation 3000). The two trajectories differ in kind, not merely in timescale; it is the signed asymmetry index, not diameter, that preserves the same trough-and-reversal shape across both treatments. The signed asymmetry index shows the same ratio (Supplementary Materials, Fig_SignedAsymmetry): redistributed $\bar{\Delta}$ reaches its minimum around generation 2400 (~400 generations post-onset) before reversing sign near generation 2680, while obstructed $\bar{\Delta}$ reaches its minimum around generation 2800 (~800 generations post-onset) and is only beginning to reverse by the end of the window. This two-fold timescale difference tracks the difference in net directional migration bias between the scenarios exactly: $m_{i\rightarrow i+1}-m_{i\leftarrow i+1}=0.03$ for redistributed versus $0.015$ for obstructed (Methods; each scenario was designed to isolate one departure from isotropic rather than to hold total migration rate fixed against the other, so total flux also differs between them—0.05 versus 0.035—and is not excluded as a contributor, though its smaller, roughly 1.4-fold difference tracks the observed timescale ratio less closely than $\Delta m$ does). Mean $|\Delta_{ij}|$ is consistent with the same picture, rising more gradually than under redistribution to a late-phase average of 0.062, roughly half the redistributed value. The asymmetry index and diameter therefore diverge in what they show about obstructed's progress toward homogenization within this window: $|\Delta_{ij}|$ and the signed asymmetry index both trace slowed, roughly twice-as-slow versions of redistributed's trajectory, but graph diameter instead rises and holds near a new plateau rather than collapsing—==perhaps because the smaller net bias and reduced total flux under obstruction are enough to reshape the graph's connectivity pattern without yet driving the chain toward the single, fully homogenized topology that redistribution reaches within this window==; the 1,000-generation forward window may simply not be long enough to see obstructed's diameter collapse the way redistributed's does.

### Divergence among redistributed lineages

Because each replicate evolves as an independent stochastic realization once asymmetric migration is imposed, individual redistributed lineages are not expected to track a single shared trajectory (Methods). Two lines of evidence bear on this directly. First, redistributed lineages lose genetic diversity at markedly different rates: the summed effective number of alleles across loci, $\sum A_e$, diverges from the isotropic band over the course of the forward phase, and several replicates approach fixation well before others (Fig_LineageDiversity). Re-indexing asymmetry against each replicate's own remaining diversity rather than generation collapses much of this spread — mean $|\Delta_{ij}|$ and signed $\bar\Delta$ both stay within the isotropic band until $\sum A_e$ falls below roughly 25–27, then rise steeply as replicates approach fixation (Fig_LineageClock), consistent with the asymmetry signal tracking how far each lineage has progressed toward fixation rather than elapsed time alone. A companion comparison against a null built from information loss alone — rebuilding isotropic graphs from random subsets of polymorphic loci — shows that information loss inflates $|\Delta_{ij}|$ by more than 10% only below an informative horizon of about 4.5 polymorphic loci per deme (Fig_InformationNull), so the late-phase rise in asymmetry is not simply an artifact of shrinking locus counts, though it does contribute once replicates approach that horizon.

Second, aligning each replicate's own trajectory to its own smoothed peak in $|\Delta_{ij}|$ (Fig_PeakAlignment) isolates the within-replicate rise from the differing timing between replicates. Individual redistributed lineages climb steadily for roughly 700 generations before reaching their own peak; peaks are identified from a smoothing spline fit to each replicate rather than its single highest window, so the climb is not an artifact of noise. Sixteen of the 50 redistributed replicates turn over before generation 2950 (median peak at generation 2869); the remaining 34 are still rising when the simulation window ends at generation 3000. The isotropic control, given the identical alignment procedure, shows only a small bump around its own alignment point, confirming that the long climb seen in redistributed replicates is real signal rather than an artifact of the alignment itself.

==Whether the decline after each replicate's peak reflects the same reorganization settling down, rather than measurement noise around an already-stable value, is not yet established: measured from each replicate's smoothed peak to generation 2999, the median decline among redistributed replicates (13%) is comparable in relative size to the decline the isotropic control shows over the same post-alignment window (17%), even though redistributed's decline is larger in absolute terms because its peak is larger. The post-peak median is also drawn from only the 16 early-turnover replicates, and is shown only where at least five replicates contribute (Fig_PeakAlignment), so it should be read as a suggestive early trend rather than a confirmed decline. The peaks themselves are also not fully separable from the information-loss caveat above: the median peak (generation 2869) falls after the median informative-horizon crossing (generation 2674), so part of the peak-then-decline pattern may reflect the last informative loci fixing rather than the underlying covariance structure alone settling toward equilibrium. Extending the redistributed simulations past generation 3000, or repeating them with mutation, would distinguish a genuine equilibrium approach from an artifact of the observation window.==

### Comparison of $\Delta_{ij}$ behavior under both scenarios

With the boundary's leaf-incident edges set aside (their large mean $|\Delta |$ ≈ 0.493 is a topological artifact, not a scenario discriminator — see Pendants, boundary effects, below), the interior edges separate the two scenarios cleanly within the roughly 254–354-generations-after-onset window: interior $\bar{\Delta}$ = -0.016 under redistributed, -0.007 under obstructed, and 0.002 under isotropic — smaller in typical magnitude (about 0.038) than the leaf edges, but the only place in the graph where scenario identity is actually distinguishable.

This interior-edge contrast also lets the two mechanisms be compared quantitatively. Redistributed and obstructed reach their asymmetry through different manipulations of the same migration matrix (Methods; Tab_ScenarioParameters): redistributed raises forward migration while holding total flux fixed at the isotropic value, whereas obstructed suppresses reverse migration while holding forward flux fixed—so the same net directional bias can arise either from redistributing a fixed budget of migration or from throttling one direction of it. Over this window, redistributed's interior $\bar\Delta$ (-0.016) is roughly 2.3 times obstructed's (-0.007), close to the two-fold difference in $\Delta m$ between the scenarios (0.03 versus 0.015) and closer to that than to the smaller, roughly 1.4-fold difference in total flux (0.05 versus 0.035)—consistent with the pattern already seen in the timing of the signed asymmetry index (Asymmetry of redistributed and obstructed connectivity, above): $\Delta m$, not total flux, is the better predictor of how strongly the two scenarios are expected to differ.

The same ordering holds for detection power. Scored against the matched isotropic null at each checkpoint (Tab_SensitivityAUC), genetic gravity's single-snapshot AUC for redistributed climbs from 0.70 at +250 generations post-onset to 0.89 at +350, while obstructed's AUC over the same interval rises only from 0.58 to 0.68 — redistributed is both a stronger and a more rapidly resolving signal than obstructed throughout the window used for the interior-edge comparison above, again consistent with its larger $\Delta m$. divMigrate, over the same checkpoints, remains at or below its symmetric-null expectation for both scenarios (Tab_SensitivityAUC), underscoring that the redistributed/obstructed power gap is a property of the underlying signal rather than of the index used to read it.

### Detection Limits

At $N=50$, the migration-ratio × $N_e$ detection grid (Methods) exposes a limit specific to Population Graph construction rather than to the index's sensitivity. A Population Graph is estimated from the conditional genetic covariance among populations; as drift drives alleles toward fixation, that covariance degenerates, retained edges drop away, and past some point the graph cannot be estimated at all. Fig_ConstructionSurvival traces this directly: constructibility holds at 100% across the entire forward window for every $N\geq 100$ cell and erodes only at $N=50$, earliest and most severely at the steepest ratio. By the final generation only 8 of 20 replicates at the 4:1 ratio still yield a graph (last valid graph at generation 2999), and 3 of 20 at 2.5:1. The failure is gradual rather than a cliff (Fig_ConstructionErosion): surviving graphs thin steadily as conditional structure is lost, so the metrics decay continuously and become undefined only once the covariance is singular. This truncation is the drift floor made visible: the generation at which construction fails is itself a structural readout of the fixation pressure the detection grid characterizes, not a nuisance to be hidden. Every cell remains fully constructible through generation 2744, which defines a common pre-fixation observation horizon; complete genotype records (200 snapshots per replicate) remain available throughout for $F_{ST}$- and allele-frequency-based analyses.

Net single-snapshot power—counting graph-construction failures as non-detections rather than dropping them—rises with both the migration asymmetry ratio and population size (Fig_StreamCPower, Tab_DetectionLimitsEndState). The $N=50$ row is limited not by the index's sensitivity but by graph construction itself: only 40% of replicates yielded a constructible graph at the 4:1 ratio, which caps attainable power there irrespective of signal strength. Among the population sizes that do reach the 80% threshold, the minimum detectable ratio is comparatively stable (2.0–2.5:1) even as the composite scale $4N_{e}\Delta m$ at those thresholds spans 6.7–34.3 (a 5.1-fold range); over this range, the detection limit tracks the migration asymmetry *ratio* more closely than the drift-relative composite—consistent with the scale-normalized construction of the asymmetry index, which reads the directional structure of the neighborhood rather than the absolute amount of differentiation drift has produced.

The end-state envelope above reads the grid at the final generation, where the $N=50$ cells are partially truncated by construction failure. To separate the method's intrinsic sensitivity from this buildability floor, the same grid was re-evaluated at the common pre-fixation horizon—the latest generation at which every cell, $N=50$ included, is still fully constructible. At that horizon there are no construction failures, so net and conditional power coincide, and every population size is scored on a complete, equally drift-advanced sample. Evaluated at generation 2744 (about 744 generations after onset), the detection limits are broadly consistent with the end state: at $N=50$, where the end-state row is capped by construction failure, the method reaches the 80% threshold at a 2.5:1 ratio on the complete sample, whereas at the end state it does not reach the threshold at any tested ratio. This places the $N=50$ shortfall at the end state with graph buildability rather than with the index's sensitivity—given a constructible graph, the directional signal is detectable at small $N_{e}$ at a ratio comparable to larger populations—and keeps the sensitivity floor (the ratio threshold) usefully distinct from the buildability floor (small-$N_{e}$ fixation) in stating the operating envelope.

### Differentiation without Direction

The framework also claims *specificity*: the asymmetry index should respond to the *direction* of gene flow, not to the *amount* of genetic structure. This was tested with a gradient of symmetric (1:1) scenarios at progressively lower migration—conditions that tip the drift–migration balance toward drift and raise neutral differentiation without imposing any direction—at two levels, beginning with the more rigorous. The self-contained statistical test asks whether the per-edge significance procedure is *calibrated*: under symmetric migration, an edge flagged as directional is a false positive, and a well-behaved test should flag such edges at the nominal rate regardless of how much neutral differentiation has accumulated. The Location test (Methods) was run on a sample of symmetric-scenario snapshots spanning the gradient, and the fraction of edges with $p<\alpha$ was recorded per snapshot; because the adjacency is held fixed and only the centroids are permuted, the geometric component of $\Delta_{ij}$ is reproduced in each edge's null, so under symmetry the empirical false-positive rate should not rise with differentiation.

Over 499 permutations per snapshot (20 snapshots per scenario), the empirical edge-level false-positive rate is essentially flat across the differentiation gradient (Tab_FPRCalibration, Fig_FPRCalibration): as mean differentiation rises about 2.1-fold from the isotropic baseline to the lowest-migration arm, the FPR at $\alpha =0.05$ stays within 0.061–0.089, and is in fact slightly lower at the most-differentiated arm. The rate does sit modestly above nominal—roughly 1.5$\times$ at $\alpha =0.05$ and somewhat more at the stringent $\alpha =0.01$ (about 2.8$\times$)—but that offset is differentiation-independent, not neutral structure being read as direction. The decisive property is the invariance: holding the adjacency fixed and permuting only the centroid identities, differentiation alone does not inflate the false-positive rate, so neutral structure—however extreme—is not manufactured into significant directional edges. The offset is not a pendant-edge artifact either: restricting to interior edges (dropping those incident to a degree-one node) leaves the $\alpha =0.05$ rate essentially unchanged at 0.061–0.089 (about 1.5$\times$ nominal), so the mild anti-conservatism is distributed across the graph rather than localized to the topologically forced leaf edges. [NOTE: original text had a `gstudio pendant argument` implementation aside here — cut during the earlier code-vs-biology pass, not restored.] The mean and median conditional distances are close (mean/median $\approx$ 1.08, Tab_FPRCalibration), so the per-edge weight distribution is roughly symmetric and edge-weight outliers are not the driver.

A second null isolates the cause of the offset. The Mechanism test—which holds the metric geometry (distances and adjacency) fixed and permutes only the node bandwidths, testing directional *alignment* rather than the presence of any structure—yields an $\alpha =0.05$ false-positive rate of 0.013–0.021 (about 0.3$\times$ nominal). It is therefore *conservative* where the Location test is anti-conservative: by conditioning on the geometry that the Location test destroys, it errs toward non-detection rather than toward false significance, the safer direction for a screening test. Neither null is exactly calibrated—the precisely calibrated reference is the empirical isotropic null used in the simulation study—but the contrast localizes the Location test's offset to its panmixia reference: removing it overshoots into conservatism rather than landing on nominal.

The same specificity is evident in the graph-mean index $\bar{\Delta}$, a descriptive complement to the calibrated per-edge test above. If the index were merely a differentiation detector, $\bar{\Delta}$ would climb with the gradient; if it is direction-specific, it should stay at the isotropic null however high differentiation rises—the "scissors" of Fig_StreamCScissors. Across the symmetric gradient, the drift–migration balance tips steadily toward drift: as the per-direction rate falls, neutral differentiation climbs sharply—mean cGD at the lowest-migration arm reaches roughly 2.6$\times$ its isotropic value—yet the *signed* mean asymmetry index never leaves the isotropic null, staying below 9e-04 across the entire gradient with no trend in sign (Tab_DifferentiationVsAsymmetry). That is the scissors the metric is designed to show: the *amount* of structure rises sharply while its *direction* does not, because symmetric migration produces no systematic neighborhood-scale imbalance for the kernel to read. The absolute index $|\bar{\Delta}|$ does creep up modestly (from 0.0047 under isotropy to about 0.0058 at the lowest-migration arm) as differentiation rises—an expected variance effect, since noisier per-edge weights inflate the magnitude of a mean that remains centered on zero—but it stays well below the levels the asymmetric scenarios reach; those scenarios depart from the null at differentiation far below what this gradient reaches, so the index responds to directionality, not to differentiation per se—the within-method counterpart of the endpoint contrast with divMigrate below.

### Pendants, boundary effects, and other marginalia issues relevant to applying $\Delta_{ij}$

The asymmetry index carries a topological component at low-degree nodes (the leaf-node effect discussed under Caveats and limitations). Because the simulated stepping stone has two degree-limited termini, two questions follow: whether that component biases the directional signal, and—since the same termini are the most drift-exposed demes in the system—whether the boundary is better read as a genuine evolutionary regime than as a numerical edge case. Both were addressed by decomposing the forward-phase snapshots by chain position, separating edges that touch a degree-one terminus from interior edges, and tracking expected heterozygosity at the ends versus the interior.

Decomposing the index by edge class separates the topological boundary effect from the directional signal (Tab_EdgeClassDecomposition). Leaf-incident edges—those touching a degree-one terminus, where one directional weight is forced to $1$—carry a large mean $|\Delta |$ (about 0.493) under both asymmetric scenarios: this is the geometry talking, not gene flow directly, and it is exactly the component the Location test reproduces in its own null and therefore calibrates away (Methods). Notably, no leaf-incident edges occur under the isotropic scenario in this dataset at any sampled generation—degree-one termini appear to be themselves a signature of the asymmetric regimes rather than a scenario-independent geometric baseline. The interior edges tell the opposite story: with the leaf edges removed, the directional signal is an interior, distributed property of the graph rather than an inflation manufactured at the boundary — the periphery contributes magnitude but no scenario discrimination. (Numbers reported under "Comparison of $\Delta_{ij}$ behavior under both scenarios," above.)

A terminus deme exchanges with a single neighbor and so receives roughly half the immigrant input of an interior deme. The simulations show exactly this (Fig_BoundaryDiversity): expected heterozygosity at the chain ends sits below the interior in every scenario and falls fastest under obstructed connectivity, where the lower total migration (0.035 versus 0.050) compounds the peripheral isolation (terminus $H_{e}$ ≈ 0.089 versus interior 0.094 by the end of the window).

## Comparison to `divMigrate`

### Isotropic (the null case)

With migration perfectly symmetric, the asymmetry index sits at its empirical near-zero baseline (mean $|\Delta |$ = 0.0329), while divMigrate reports a substantially larger residual directional signal (mean $|A|$ = 0.053, about 1.6$\times$ larger) that arises from drift- and $N_{e}$-driven differentiation rather than genuine direction. This isotropic behavior is the reference both scenarios below are scored against, and the gap it exposes is the subject of Specificity, below.

### Obstructed

Endpoint concordance is positive here (median Spearman $\rho$ = 0.036, Tab_GravityVsDivMigrate). Genetic gravity separates this slower-developing scenario from the isotropic null more weakly (AUC 0.502, near chance at onset but rising thereafter; power 0.16), while divMigrate again fails to discriminate (AUC 0.34, power 0.04). At late generations, sustained directional drift under this scenario drives near-complete allele fixation in a subset of replicates (~75% of loci fixed in affected replicates versus 63% in unaffected replicates at the same generation), collapsing the differentiation divMigrate's normalization depends on: 38% of its aggregated time-points diverge from the gravity-index display range by orders of magnitude, up to $\sim {10}^{13}$ (Fig_DivMigrateTimecourse) — a failure mode induced by the asymmetry itself, not incidental noise. The topology-derived index is structurally insulated from this feedback, deriving as it does from conditional independence structure rather than any differentiation ratio.

### Specificity

The isotropic-null gap above is diagnostic, not incidental: divMigrate's directional signal is governed by how much differentiation drift has produced, not by whether that differentiation is directional — the same failure that produces its normalization blow-up under sustained asymmetry (Obstructed, above). The topology-derived index is the less prone of the two to mistaking nondirectional differentiation for directional flow, which is the property that matters most when the analyst does not already know whether asymmetry is present.

### Bandwidth sensitivity

*(moved verbatim from "## Bandwidth sensitivity")*

The bandwidth estimator $b_{i}=s_{i}/k_{i}$ is a modeling choice; the load-bearing question is not its absolute scale but whether the *sign* and *rank order* of $\Delta_{ij}$—which edge flows which way, and which edges carry the strongest signal—survive a deliberately non-local choice. $\Delta$ was recomputed on the base-study forward snapshots under three departures from the canonical local bandwidth and compared, edge by edge, to it: a fixed *global* bandwidth (a single scale, the mean retained edge weight, for every node); the local bandwidth *scaled* by $\frac{1}{2}$ and by $2$ (a sharper and a flatter kernel); and a *perplexity*-based per-node bandwidth (a tSNE-style $\sigma$ search). All were evaluated on the comparable $w_{i\rightarrow j}-w_{j\rightarrow i}$ scale, recording, against the canonical index and over the directional scenarios, the fraction of edges with the same sign, the Spearman correlation of the signed $\Delta$ and of $|\Delta |$, and whether the graph-mean $\bar{\Delta}$ keeps its sign.

The result is more nuanced than a clean robustness verdict, and it separates the aggregate from the edge level (Tab_BandwidthSensitivity). Edge-level direction is only *moderately* preserved: across the four non-local alternatives, the sign of $\Delta_{ij}$ agrees with the canonical local estimator on 75–84% of edges—so roughly one edge in five changes orientation—and the signed rank order correlates at Spearman $\rho =0.38$–0.78, weakest for the perplexity kernel (a genuinely different construction rather than a rescaling) and imperfect even for the mild $\times \frac{1}{2}$ and $\times 2$ rescalings. The graph-mean $\bar{\Delta}$ is steadier: its sign is preserved across the alternatives in 87% of redistributed and 84% of obstructed snapshots, the aggregate orientation surviving bandwidth changes that reshuffle individual edges. The bandwidth is therefore a genuine modeling choice at the edge level, not an immaterial convenience—changing it both rescales magnitudes and reorders and reorients a meaningful minority of edges—while what is robust is the *aggregate* directional signal, $\bar{\Delta}$ and the longitudinal graph-metric trajectories on which the conclusions rest. Individual-edge orientations are bandwidth-sensitive and should be read as exploratory, consistent with the edge non-independence and degree dependence documented below and in Caveats and limitations. The local estimator remains the principled default (scale-equivariant and parameter-free), but it earns that status by argument rather than by the edge-level invariance this check was expected to show.
