## The Evolution of asymmetry in genetic topologies

### Isotropic baseline

Individual-based simulations were run to establish that the burn-in period produces stable, comparable starting topologies before any directional treatment is applied. Dyer (2007) showed that mean degree centrality ($\bar{C}_D$) and graph diameter track standard population-genetic measures of structure and gene flow ($F_{ST}$ and Slatkin's $\hat{M}$); here, where the focus is the graph's topology itself rather than that correspondence, the same two metrics serve as a check that replicate runs have converged to equilibrium. Across the isotropic replicates, $\bar{C}_D$ falls and graph diameter rises from their initial values, each relaxing toward a stable plateau within the 2,000-generation burn-in and remaining flat through the subsequent isotropic forward phase (Fig_IsotropicBaseline). The magnitude of the asymmetry index, $\overline{|\Delta_{ij}|}$, follows the same relaxation on the same timescale—rising from near zero toward its own plateau, despite no directional gene flow being present in this scenario (Discussion). The signed mean, by contrast, remains centered on zero throughout the simulation (not shown; forward-phase $\bar{\Delta}$ = 0.0014, 95% CI 0.0003–0.0025), confirming that the isotropic replicates carry no net directional bias. Every replicate therefore enters the forward phase—and the redistributed and obstructed treatments that follow—from a topologically equilibrated, directionally unbiased, and directly comparable starting configuration, establishing the isotropic scenario as the empirical null against which asymmetry in the other two scenarios is judged.

### Asymmetry with redistributed and obstructed connectivity

Trend lines shown for the forward phase (Fig_ScenarioComparison; Fig_SignedAsymmetry in Supplementary Materials) are penalized regression splines fit by REML (Methods), rather than the parametric exponential-equilibrium curve used for the isotropic burn-in (Fig_IsotropicBaseline): the observed trajectories — a sigmoidal diameter decline and biphasic signed $\bar{\Delta}$ under redistributed, and a diameter that rises before easing only late in the window under obstructed — are not captured by a single parametric family across all three (fitting diagnostics in Supplementary Materials). The isotropic reference shown alongside each scenario is the burn-in's exponential-equilibrium fit (Fig_IsotropicBaseline), extrapolated across the forward phase.

Under the redistributed treatment, the topological signature of directional migration emerges within the forward phase itself, well before the terminal snapshot (Fig_ScenarioComparison, left). $\bar{C}_D$ and graph diameter, both flat through the isotropic forward phase (Isotropic baseline), diverge from the isotropic reference soon after treatment begins: over the final 50 snapshots (generations 2950–2999), $\bar{C}_D$ averages 0.270 against an isotropic 0.318, and diameter averages 7.90 — roughly half the isotropic 16.14 — following a sigmoidal decline. The asymmetry index tracks the same divergence: mean $|\Delta_{ij}|$ remains within the isotropic band through the early redistributed phase before climbing steadily to a late-phase average of 0.133 (isotropic: 0.034), roughly four-fold. This combination — rising asymmetry alongside a collapsing diameter and falling $\bar{C}_D$ — is examined against the homogenization expectation from Methods in Discussion.

Under the obstructed treatment the asymmetry index also rises, but on this window's evidence, more slowly than under redistribution (Fig_ScenarioComparison, right). $\bar{C}_D$ declines in a similar fashion to redistributed (late-phase average 0.259 against isotropic 0.318). Graph diameter behaves qualitatively differently from redistributed, rather than tracing a delayed version of the same shape: redistributed diameter stays near the isotropic level until about generation 2350, then collapses sharply to roughly 7, while obstructed diameter instead rises substantially — to roughly 19.5 — around generation 2500–2750, holds near that peak, and only eases slightly after generation 2800 (late-phase average 18.37 against isotropic 16.14, still declining at generation 3000). The two trajectories differ in kind, not merely in timescale; it is the signed asymmetry index, not diameter, that preserves the same trough-and-reversal shape across both treatments. The signed asymmetry index shows the same ratio (Supplementary Materials, Fig_SignedAsymmetry): redistributed $\bar{\Delta}$ reaches its minimum around generation 2400 (~400 generations post-onset) before reversing sign near generation 2680, while obstructed $\bar{\Delta}$ reaches its minimum around generation 2800 (~800 generations post-onset) and is only beginning to reverse by the end of the window. This two-fold timescale difference tracks the difference in net directional migration bias between the scenarios exactly: $m_{i\rightarrow i+1}-m_{i\leftarrow i+1}=0.03$ for redistributed versus $0.015$ for obstructed (Methods; each scenario was designed to isolate one departure from isotropic rather than to hold total migration rate fixed against the other, so total flux also differs between them—0.05 versus 0.035—and is not excluded as a contributor, though its smaller, roughly 1.4-fold difference tracks the observed timescale ratio less closely than $\Delta m$ does). Mean $|\Delta_{ij}|$ is consistent with the same picture, rising more gradually than under redistribution to a late-phase average of 0.062, roughly half the redistributed value. The asymmetry index and diameter therefore diverge in what they show about obstructed's progress toward homogenization within this window: $|\Delta_{ij}|$ and the signed asymmetry index both trace slowed, roughly twice-as-slow versions of redistributed's trajectory, but graph diameter instead rises and holds near a new plateau rather than collapsing; whether this reflects a genuinely different endpoint or simply a longer window is taken up in Discussion (Caveats and limitations).

### Divergence among redistributed lineages

Because each replicate evolves as an independent stochastic realization once asymmetric migration is imposed, individual redistributed lineages are not expected to track a single shared trajectory (Methods). Two lines of evidence bear on this directly. First, redistributed lineages lose genetic diversity at markedly different rates: the summed effective number of alleles across loci, $\sum A_e$, diverges from the isotropic band over the course of the forward phase, and several replicates approach fixation well before others (Fig_LineageDiversity). Re-indexing asymmetry against each replicate's own remaining diversity rather than generation collapses much of this spread — mean $|\Delta_{ij}|$ and signed $\bar\Delta$ both stay within the isotropic band until $\sum A_e$ falls below roughly 25–27, then rise steeply as replicates approach fixation (Fig_LineageClock), consistent with the asymmetry signal tracking how far each lineage has progressed toward fixation rather than elapsed time alone. A companion comparison against a null built from information loss alone — rebuilding isotropic graphs from random subsets of polymorphic loci — shows that information loss inflates $|\Delta_{ij}|$ by more than 10% only below an informative horizon of about 4.5 polymorphic loci per deme (Fig_InformationNull), so the late-phase rise in asymmetry is not simply an artifact of shrinking locus counts, though it does contribute once replicates approach that horizon.

Second, aligning each replicate's own trajectory to its own smoothed peak in $|\Delta_{ij}|$ (Fig_PeakAlignment) isolates the within-replicate rise from the differing timing between replicates. Individual redistributed lineages climb steadily for roughly 700 generations before reaching their own peak; peaks are identified from a smoothing spline fit to each replicate rather than its single highest window, so the climb is not an artifact of noise. Sixteen of the 50 redistributed replicates turn over before generation 2950 (median peak at generation 2869); the remaining 34 are still rising when the simulation window ends at generation 3000. The isotropic control, given the identical alignment procedure, shows only a small bump around its own alignment point, confirming that the long climb seen in redistributed replicates is real signal rather than an artifact of the alignment itself.

Measured from each replicate's smoothed peak to generation 2999, the median decline among redistributed replicates is 13%, comparable in relative size to the 17% decline the isotropic control shows over the same post-alignment window, though redistributed's decline is larger in absolute terms because its peak is larger. The post-peak median is drawn from only the 16 early-turnover replicates and is shown only where at least five replicates contribute (Fig_PeakAlignment). The median peak (generation 2869) falls after the median informative-horizon crossing (generation 2674, Fig_InformationNull). Whether the decline is distinguishable from noise, and what would be needed to establish that it is, is taken up in Discussion (Caveats and limitations).

### Bandwidth sensitivity

*(moved verbatim from "## Bandwidth sensitivity")*

The bandwidth estimator $b_{i}=s_{i}/k_{i}$ is a modeling choice (Methods); the question here is not its absolute scale but whether the *sign* and *rank order* of $\Delta_{ij}$—which edge flows which way, and which edges carry the strongest signal—survive a deliberately non-local choice. $\Delta$ was recomputed under each of the alternative bandwidths (Methods) and compared, edge by edge, to the canonical local estimator on the comparable $w_{i\rightarrow j}-w_{j\rightarrow i}$ scale: the fraction of edges with the same sign, the Spearman correlation of the signed $\Delta$ and of $|\Delta |$, and whether the graph-mean $\bar{\Delta}$ keeps its sign, all recorded against the canonical index over the directional scenarios.

The result separates the aggregate from the edge level (Tab_BandwidthSensitivity). Edge-level direction is only *moderately* preserved: across the four non-local alternatives, the sign of $\Delta_{ij}$ agrees with the canonical local estimator on 75–84% of edges—so roughly one edge in five changes orientation—and the signed rank order correlates at Spearman $\rho =0.38$–0.78, weakest for the perplexity kernel (a genuinely different construction rather than a rescaling) and imperfect even for the mild $\times \frac{1}{2}$ and $\times 2$ rescalings. The graph-mean $\bar{\Delta}$ is steadier: its sign is preserved across the alternatives in 87% of redistributed and 84% of obstructed snapshots. What these numbers mean for how edge-level versus aggregate results should be read is taken up in Discussion (Caveats and limitations).
